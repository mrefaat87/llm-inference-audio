Alex: So Jenny, I want to start with a number that should make you uncomfortable. Imagine I tell you my inference engine has a P99 latency of 200 milliseconds. Sounds great, right?

Jenny: Yeah, that's a fine number. I'd ship that.

Alex: Now what if I told you I measured it with fifty concurrent clients in a closed loop, and the same engine, under the same actual request rate from real users, has a P99 of two seconds?

Jenny: Wait, ten times worse? Same hardware, same engine, same QPS?

Alex: Same everything. The only thing that changed is how I generated the load. And this is the thing nobody warns you about when you start benchmarking LLMs — the methodology is the result. The number you report is more about how you measured than what you measured.

Jenny: Okay, you have to unpack that. Closed loop versus what?

Alex: Open loop. Closed loop is: I have N worker threads, each one sends a request, waits for the full response, sends the next. So there are always exactly N requests in flight. Ever.

Jenny: Right, that's how every load test I've ever written for an internal service works. JMeter, locust with users — same pattern.

Alex: Exactly. And here's the problem. When the system slows down, what happens to your offered load?

Jenny: It... drops. Because each thread is stuck waiting longer before it sends the next one. Oh. Oh, that's bad.

Alex: That's catastrophically bad for capacity planning. Real users don't behave that way. If your service goes from 50 milliseconds to 5 seconds, your users don't politely stop sending requests. They keep clicking. New users keep arriving. The Poisson process of arrivals doesn't care that your GPU is on fire.

Jenny: So closed-loop is basically a thermostat. The hotter it gets, the less load you apply. Which means you literally cannot measure overload behavior.

Alex: You cannot measure it. By construction. And there's a worse version of this called coordinated omission. Picture this: closed loop, one request takes five seconds instead of fifty milliseconds. During those five seconds, your benchmark records exactly one measurement — the slow one.

Jenny: But in production, during those same five seconds, a hundred more requests would have arrived, and every single one of them would also see multi-second latency.

Alex: And none of those hundred measurements exist in your dataset. Your P99 is computed over a sample that systematically excludes the worst cases. Gil Tene has been screaming about this for fifteen years and people still don't get it.

Jenny: So the fix is open loop. Fixed arrival rate, Poisson-distributed, send requests on schedule no matter what.

Alex: Right. vLLM's benchmark_serving in Poisson mode does this correctly. MLPerf's server scenario does this correctly. If your tool doesn't, throw it out.

Jenny: Okay so I've got the load generation right. Now what am I actually measuring? Because TTFT, TPOT, ITL, TBT, E2E — there's like seven acronyms and I lose track.

Alex: Yeah, let's anchor it. Three numbers tell the story. TTFT — time to first token — is your prefill latency. That's the "did the page load" feeling. TPOT — time per output token — is your steady-state decode speed. That's "is it still typing." And E2E is the whole request.

Jenny: And percentiles for all of them.

Alex: Always percentiles. Never the mean. LLM latency distributions are violently right-skewed — most requests cluster around the median, then there's this long tail of slow ones. The mean is dragged up by the tail and tells you nothing about typical experience. The median tells you nothing about the tail.

Jenny: So you need both. P50 for the typical user, P95 and P99 for the SLO.

Alex: And here's the rule that catches people: if your P95 is five times your P50, you have a queueing problem or a batching problem. That ratio is diagnostic. Healthy systems have tight distributions. If it's five-x, you're either thrashing the scheduler or your batches are getting stuck behind a long-decode prisoner.

Jenny: That's a great heuristic. Like watching a CloudWatch dashboard where the P50 and P99 are walking together versus diverging — divergence means something's queued up.

Alex: Exactly. Now there's one more metric that matters more than any of these, and it's the one nobody reports. Goodput.

Jenny: Goodput as in — throughput minus the ones that violated SLO?

Alex: Yes. Requests-meeting-SLO over total requests, times the throughput. Because Goodhart's Law is real — the moment you make raw tokens-per-second your target, your engineers will optimize tokens-per-second. They'll crank batch size, latency goes to hell, P99 doubles, but the dashboard goes up and to the right.

Jenny: And you ship a regression because the metric was a target. Same thing happens when teams optimize for CPU utilization without watching latency — you can pin a fleet at 95% CPU and call it efficient, and customers are timing out.

Alex: Goodput bakes the SLO into the metric. You can't game it without actually being faster for users. That's the antidote.

Jenny: Okay, methodology, metrics. How do I run the test? Just pick a rate and go?

Alex: No. Graduated ramp. Start low — five requests per second — hold for two minutes, step up by five, hold, step up, hold. Plot latency against throughput at every step.

Jenny: Oh, this is exactly a step-scaling stress test. You're finding the knee of the curve.

Alex: That's the whole point. You'll see three regions. Linear region — latency flat, lots of headroom. The knee — latency starts climbing, this is your real capacity. And the overload region — latency explodes, throughput sometimes actually decreases because the scheduler is thrashing.

Jenny: Right, you'd never set target tracking on auto scaling without first knowing what offered load corresponds to your target utilization. The ramp gives you the load-to-utilization curve. Without it you're just guessing.

Alex: And every benchmark report should publish that curve, not a single number.

Jenny: Okay, what trips people up?

Alex: Cold cache is the classic. First fifty requests are always slow — CUDA kernel compilation, KV blocks getting allocated, the autotuner picking GEMM shapes. Discard those. Synthetic prompts are the next one — "write a 500-word essay" produces really uniform output lengths, which is nothing like real traffic.

Jenny: Real traffic is heavy-tailed on output length too?

Alex: Wildly. You'll have a thousand requests that generate 50 tokens and then one that generates 8000. And that one request sits in the batch for the entire duration, blocking slots, and it absolutely dominates the tail. If your benchmark has fixed max_tokens, you've erased that entire failure mode.

Jenny: So real prompts, real length distribution. Which is why people use ShareGPT.

Alex: ShareGPT is the floor. The ceiling is shadow traffic. Mirror real production requests at your candidate config, throw away the response, compare the metrics. That's the only benchmark that actually transfers to production.

Jenny: Because it preserves everything — the input length distribution, the output length distribution, the parameter mix, the tenant mix, the temporal bursts.

Alex: All five dimensions. The mean ISL doesn't matter — the histogram matters. A workload that's 50% short prompts and 50% giant ones behaves nothing like a uniform workload at the same average length. The scheduler sees a totally different shape.

Jenny: This is why I always pushed back when people would say "we tested with average request size." Average is a lie when the distribution is bimodal.

Alex: And one more thing — please don't conflate benchmarks with evals. Benchmarks tell you how fast and cheap. Evals tell you how good the outputs are. Quantization can make your benchmark look amazing and silently tank your accuracy on math problems. You always pair a serving change with at least a smoke eval.

Jenny: Otherwise you ship a faster engine that's also dumber and you don't find out until customer complaints.

Alex: Exactly. Speed without quality held constant is not a win.

Jenny: Okay so where does this lead us? We've got methodology, we've got metrics, we've got the ramp.

Alex: Next we connect this to money. Because the whole point of benchmarking is figuring out what a token costs you, and what an SLO-compliant token costs you, and whether the engine choice you just made actually penciled out at your traffic mix.

Jenny: Cost economics. Cost per token is the new cost per request.

Alex: And once you have goodput at a target SLO, you can finally divide dollars per GPU-hour by SLO-compliant tokens per second and get a number that means something. That's where we're going next — turning these latency curves into a unit economics model. Because eventually somebody from finance is going to ask why the inference bill doubled, and "we improved tokens per second by 40%" is not the answer they want.

Jenny: They want dollars per million tokens. At P99. On real traffic.

Alex: That's the only number that matters in the end. We'll get there next episode.
