Alex: So today we're talking about benchmarking LLM inference, and honestly, this is where most people get it completely wrong.
Jenny: Bold claim. What do you mean wrong?
Alex: Okay so first, let's talk about what you should even measure. You've got TTFT — time to first token — which tells you how fast the system starts responding. Then TBT, time between tokens, which is your streaming smoothness. And then there's end-to-end latency, throughput, all that. But here's the thing — you need to report percentiles, not averages.
Jenny: Why not averages? They seem straightforward.
Alex: Because a system with 50 millisecond average latency but a 5-second P99 is way worse than one with 80 millisecond average and 120 millisecond P99. The average hides the fact that one in a hundred users is waiting 5 seconds. Percentiles tell you the real story.
Jenny: Okay that makes sense. So what's the big mistake people make in how they actually run benchmarks?
Alex: Open-loop versus closed-loop. This is the critical distinction and most people use closed-loop without even realizing it. In closed-loop, you send a request, wait for it to finish, then send the next one. So if the system slows down, your request rate automatically drops. You're being nice to the system.
Jenny: And that's not how real traffic works.
Alex: Exactly! In production, users don't wait for other users' requests to finish. They just keep coming. That's open-loop — you send requests at a fixed rate regardless of how the system is doing. When the system slows down, requests pile up, and you see the real queueing latency.
Jenny: So how different are the results?
Alex: Night and day. The same system might show 200 millisecond P99 in closed-loop but 2000 milliseconds in open-loop. Ten times worse. The difference is all queueing delay that closed-loop hides by construction.
Jenny: Wait, that's a massive difference. So every closed-loop benchmark I've seen is basically lying to me?
Alex: Not lying, but definitely painting a rosier picture. There's actually a name for this problem — coordinated omission. Say a request takes 5 seconds instead of 50 milliseconds. In closed-loop, your benchmark just... pauses. No measurements during those 5 seconds. But in reality, maybe a hundred users would have sent requests during that time, all experiencing multi-second latency. Your benchmark records one slow measurement. Reality would record a hundred.
Jenny: Oh wow, so P99 is drastically underreported. That's sneaky.
Alex: Right. The fix is simple — use open-loop. vLLM's benchmark serving script has a Poisson mode that avoids this by design.
Jenny: Okay so open-loop, report percentiles. How should I actually structure the test?
Alex: Graduated ramps. Don't just benchmark at one flat rate. Start at 5 requests per second, bump it up by 5 every couple minutes, and plot latency versus throughput at each step. You'll see three regions — a linear region where latency is flat and the system has headroom, then the knee where latency starts climbing, and then the overload region where everything falls apart.
Jenny: That knee is basically your real capacity.
Alex: Exactly. Think of it like a step-scaling stress test in AWS. You wouldn't set target tracking to 80% CPU without first knowing what load corresponds to 80%. The ramp gives you that curve.
Jenny: What are the gotchas people miss even when they do all this right?
Alex: Oh there are several. Cold cache is a big one — your first requests are always slower because of CUDA compilation and memory allocation. You need to send at least 50 warmup requests and throw those measurements away. Then there's synthetic prompts — if you just say "write a 500-word essay," every response is about the same length. Real traffic has wildly variable output lengths. Use something like ShareGPT data or production replays.
Jenny: What about hardware effects?
Alex: GPU thermal throttling. People forget that GPUs get hot and throttle themselves. A 10-minute benchmark might look great, but a 2-hour benchmark shows throughput slowly dropping as the GPU heats up. You need to monitor temperature.
Jenny: And there's also a metric you mentioned — goodput? That sounds important.
Alex: It might be the most important one. Goodput is SLO-compliant throughput — how many requests actually met your latency target divided by total requests. You could have amazing average throughput, but if half your requests miss the SLO, your goodput is terrible. That's the metric that actually tells you if your system works for users.
Jenny: So to sum it up — open-loop, percentiles, graduated ramps, real data, and measure goodput. That's the recipe.
Alex: That's the recipe. Get any one of those wrong and your benchmark is basically fiction.
