Alex: So Jenny, I want to ask you something. When you were designing auto scaling policies at AWS, did you ever design the scaling policy first and then figure out what instance types you'd run on later?

Jenny: God no. That would be insane. The whole policy depends on the instance — how fast it boots, how much capacity it adds, what the cost curve looks like. You design them together.

Alex: Right. So here's what's wild about the history of AI research. For basically a decade, people designed model architectures to maximize benchmark scores, and then figured out how to serve them later. Like designing your scaling policy in a vacuum and hoping it works on whatever hardware shows up.

Jenny: That sounds like it worked fine until it didn't.

Alex: Exactly. And the "didn't" moment is basically now. Frontier labs — the ones building the biggest models — have completely abandoned that separation. They're now designing the model architecture, the hardware target, and the serving infrastructure as one integrated system. They're calling it co-design, and it's fundamentally changing what inference infrastructure looks like.

Jenny: Okay, I get the principle. But give me something concrete. What does a co-design decision actually look like in practice?

Alex: Perfect example. Moonshot AI released a model called Kimi K2 in 2025, and they did something unusual — they actually published a blog post explaining why they made a specific architectural tradeoff. They deliberately reduced the number of attention heads in the model.

Jenny: Wait, attention heads are the core compute mechanism, right? That's like saying "we deliberately reduced the number of engines on our plane."

Alex: Great instinct, but here's where it gets interesting. They didn't just remove capacity — they moved it. They reduced attention heads and simultaneously increased the number of experts in their mixture-of-experts setup. Same total model knowledge, but distributed differently. And the reason comes down to one thing: how each mechanism scales with context length.

Jenny: Okay, you need to unpack that. Why does it matter which mechanism holds the knowledge?

Alex: Because attention and experts have completely different cost curves. Think about it like two different AWS resource types. Attention scales quadratically with context length. If your sequence is twice as long, attention doesn't cost twice as much — it costs four times as much. It's O(n²).

Jenny: That's like... okay, that's like cross-AZ traffic in a mesh topology. Double the nodes, quadruple the connections.

Alex: That's actually a perfect analogy. Every token has to attend to every other token, just like every node in a full mesh has to talk to every other node. Now, experts are completely different. Each expert processes a fixed amount of compute per token regardless of how long the sequence is. It's constant cost per token. More like a stateless microservice — doesn't matter how many requests came before yours, your request costs the same to process.

Jenny: Oh. So they're literally reshaping the cost curve of the model. Moving capacity from the quadratic mechanism to the constant-cost mechanism.

Alex: Exactly. And think about what that means at their target context lengths. Kimi K2 is designed for 128k token contexts. At that length, if you have 64 attention heads instead of 32, you're not just doing twice the work — you're doing twice the work at 128,000 squared scale. The absolute compute difference is enormous. But swapping that capacity into more experts? The experts don't care if you're at 1k tokens or 128k tokens. Same cost.

Jenny: So the decision of "how many attention heads should this model have" isn't a research question anymore. It's a serving economics question.

Alex: That is the key mental shift. You answer that question by looking at: what's our target context length distribution in production, what's our expected request volume, and what does the serving cost per request look like under different head counts. It's capacity planning. It's infrastructure economics.

Jenny: Hang on though. You said they keep the number of activated experts per token constant, but they increase the total number of experts. Why? If only the same number fire per token, what does having more total experts buy you?

Alex: Knowledge capacity. Think of it like having a bigger fleet of specialized instance types. On any given request, you still route to the same number of instances — say, eight. But if your fleet has 256 instance types instead of 64, you can have much more specialized instances. Each one is an expert at a narrower domain. The per-request cost is the same, but the total knowledge the system can express is much larger.

Jenny: That's sparse activation. You're paying for breadth of capability without paying for it on every single request.

Alex: Exactly. And this is why the tradeoff works — fewer attention heads cuts your context-scaling cost, more sparse experts maintains or even increases your knowledge capacity, and the serving bill goes down at long contexts. It's not a compromise. It's a genuine Pareto improvement, but only if you design for it from the start.

Jenny: Okay so that's the model architecture dimension. You said there are other dimensions being co-designed simultaneously?

Alex: Two more, and they're just as important. The second is hardware target. Frontier models are now designed for a specific GPU generation. If you're training on H100s and you know you'll serve on H100s, you design the model to use FP8 natively — because H100 Tensor Cores have native FP8 support that gives you roughly two-times compute throughput. You also design your parallelism strategy around NVLink topology specifically, rather than trying to be hardware-agnostic.

Jenny: So it's like writing your application to exploit specific EC2 instance features — placement groups, EBS optimized paths, Nitro offloads — instead of writing generic code and hoping the instance is fast enough.

Alex: Exactly right. A model trained specifically for FP8 on H100 will have learned to be robust to that precision level during training. You don't lose quality at inference because the model never knew anything different. Versus taking a model trained in BF16 and quantizing it to FP8 after the fact — that's a lossy conversion.

Jenny: What's the third dimension?

Alex: Context and harness. This is the sneaky one. Labs are now training models with the specific tool-calling schemas and agentic workflows the model will actually use in production. The model learns during training how to optimally use its tools, rather than adapting to them at inference time.

Jenny: Oh, that's interesting. So instead of training a general-purpose model and then prompt-engineering it to use your tool format...

Alex: You bake the tool format into training. The model learns the schema, learns when tools are useful, learns how to format calls efficiently. It improves both quality and efficiency — fewer wasted tokens on malformed tool calls, fewer retries, less prompt overhead.

Jenny: Okay, I want to come back to something. You mentioned attention's quadratic cost is the reason context length has basically stalled at about a million tokens. But I thought FlashAttention solved the attention scaling problem?

Alex: Common misconception, and an important one. FlashAttention solved the memory scaling problem. Standard attention used to require O(n²) memory to store the full attention matrix. FlashAttention computes attention tile by tile, so memory scales linearly. But the compute is still quadratic. You still have to calculate every pairwise attention score. FlashAttention just does it more efficiently, without materializing the full matrix in memory.

Jenny: So FlashAttention is like... streaming a large dataset through a fixed-memory buffer instead of loading it all into RAM. You solved the memory wall, but you still have to process every byte.

Alex: Perfect. And that's why we've been stuck near a million tokens for about two years. At a million tokens on a large model, attention already dominates compute. Going to ten million tokens would mean roughly a hundred times more attention computation. That's just not feasible on current hardware at any reasonable cost.

Jenny: So what's the path forward? If you can't brute-force past the quadratic wall, what do you do?

Alex: Exactly what co-design predicts. You replace attention at some layers with mechanisms that scale linearly — state space models like Mamba, or hybrid architectures that use attention selectively at only certain layers instead of every layer. You keep attention where it matters most and use cheaper alternatives elsewhere.

Jenny: So the hardware constraint is literally shaping what model architectures look like at the frontier. The reason some models are hybrids isn't because researchers thought hybrids were elegant — it's because quadratic attention can't scale to the context lengths the market demands.

Alex: That's it. And this is what makes infrastructure engineering for AI genuinely different from classical systems work. In traditional infrastructure, you pick the best available system and optimize around it. In AI inference, the system itself is being designed around your infrastructure constraints. The model architecture is the infrastructure decision.

Jenny: So looking forward — where does this go?

Alex: This is where it gets really exciting. The co-design trend means infrastructure engineers are now in the room when model architecture decisions are made. The next frontier is disaggregated inference — separating prefill and decode onto different hardware because they have fundamentally different compute profiles. And that's another co-design decision. If you know your model will run on a disaggregated system, you can design the architecture to exploit that split.

Jenny: So we're heading toward a world where you can't really separate "model research" from "infrastructure engineering" at all. They're the same discipline.

Alex: They're converging, fast. And for someone coming from infrastructure — understanding cost curves, scaling properties, hardware constraints — you're not behind. You're actually bringing exactly the mental models that matter most right now. The question "how many attention heads should this model have" is answered with the same thinking as "what instance type mix should this fleet use." Different domain, same engineering discipline.

Jenny: I love that framing. Next time, we should dig into what disaggregated inference actually looks like in practice — prefill versus decode separation and why that split matters.

Alex: Absolutely. And we'll talk about how that connects to the scheduling and routing problems we've covered before. Because once you split prefill and decode, you've got a distributed systems routing problem that would feel very familiar to anyone who's built request routing at scale.
