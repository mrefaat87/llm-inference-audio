Alex: So Jenny, I want to try something today. Imagine you're standing in front of a whiteboard, and someone asks you: "We need to serve a 70-billion parameter language model to ten thousand users. Pick your infrastructure." What's the first thing you think about?

Jenny: Honestly? It's the same question I'd ask about any workload. What are my constraints? Latency targets, throughput requirements, operational complexity my team can handle. And then I'd pick the compute abstraction that matches — like choosing between ECS, EKS, and Lambda. Each one makes real tradeoffs.

Alex: That is exactly the right frame, because that's precisely what's happening in the inference engine space right now. You've got four serious contenders — vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang, and Dynamo — and each one is basically a different philosophy about how to serve models. They're not just different codebases. They're different opinions about what matters most.

Jenny: Okay, so walk me through the philosophies. Because when I hear "four options," my instinct is — one of these is the default, and the others exist because the default breaks for specific workloads.

Alex: You nailed it. vLLM is the default. It's the Linux of LLM inference — that's not my analogy, that's basically how the community thinks about it. It came out of UC Berkeley in 2023, it's open-source, community-driven, and it prioritizes flexibility and broad support over squeezing out every last drop of performance. It runs on NVIDIA, AMD, even Intel GPUs. It supports the widest set of models. If you don't know what you need yet, you start here.

Jenny: So it's on-demand EC2. The safe starting point. You know it works, you know the ecosystem, and you optimize later.

Alex: Exactly. Now TensorRT-LLM is the opposite end of the spectrum. It's NVIDIA's engine, and its entire philosophy is: maximum performance on NVIDIA hardware, period. It takes your model and does ahead-of-time compilation — it literally compiles the model into an optimized TensorRT engine. Custom fused kernels, hardware-specific optimizations, the whole works.

Jenny: Wait, ahead-of-time compilation? So you're building a machine image, basically. Like baking an AMI instead of running a general-purpose instance and installing at boot.

Alex: That's a perfect analogy. And just like baking an AMI, there's a build step. For a 70B model, that compilation takes thirty to sixty minutes. But once it's built, you get ten to thirty percent more throughput than vLLM on the same hardware.

Jenny: Okay but that build step is a real cost. What happens when you want to swap models? Or update to a new version?

Alex: You rebuild. Every time. And that's exactly why it requires a dedicated MLOps team to operate. The performance is real — we're talking maybe 2,500 to 5,000 output tokens per second on H100s for a 70B model, compared to vLLM's 2,000 to 4,000. But the operational complexity is genuinely higher. You need to version those compiled engines, manage the build pipeline, deal with the fact that a compiled engine is locked to a specific GPU architecture.

Jenny: So this is reserved instances with Savings Plans. You get better unit economics, but you're committing, and you need the operational maturity to manage that commitment.

Alex: I love that. Yes. And here's the thing — NVIDIA packages TensorRT-LLM inside their NIM containers, so if you're already in the NVIDIA ecosystem, the operational burden drops a lot. But if you're rolling your own, it's real work.

Jenny: Alright, so vLLM is the general-purpose default, TensorRT-LLM is the NVIDIA-optimized power play. What about SGLang? Where does that fit?

Alex: SGLang is the interesting one. It also came out of UC Berkeley — in fact, one of the co-creators, Lianmin Zheng, was also a co-creator of vLLM. But SGLang started with a very different problem. Its original killer feature is structured generation — when you need the model to output valid JSON, or follow a specific schema, or fill in a template.

Jenny: How much of a difference are we talking?

Alex: Two to five X faster than vLLM for structured output. And the way it achieves that is genuinely clever — it uses a zero-overhead finite state machine approach. But here's what happened over time: SGLang got so good at the general case too that it's now within five to ten percent of vLLM on standard workloads. And for certain workloads, it's actually faster.

Jenny: Okay, hang on. Why would an engine built for structured generation end up competitive on general workloads? That seems like it shouldn't happen.

Alex: Great question. It comes down to a core architectural choice called RadixAttention. So you know how vLLM uses PagedAttention — it treats GPU memory like virtual memory pages to avoid wasting KV cache space?

Jenny: Right, that's the OS virtual memory analogy. The KV cache is your warm instance pool, and PagedAttention lets you pack it efficiently instead of pre-allocating worst-case memory for every request.

Alex: Exactly. SGLang took a different approach. RadixAttention organizes the KV cache as a radix tree — a prefix tree. So when multiple requests share the same system prompt, or the same conversation prefix, or the same few-shot examples, SGLang automatically detects that and shares the cached computation.

Jenny: Oh, that's like a content-addressable cache. If I've already computed the attention for "You are a helpful assistant that outputs JSON," and a thousand requests start with that same prefix, I compute it once and share it.

Alex: Precisely. And this gives you thirty to eighty percent reduction in time-to-first-token for shared-prefix workloads. Think about multi-turn conversations where every message includes the full conversation history. Each new turn shares a massive prefix with the previous turn.

Jenny: That's huge. So if I'm running a chatbot or an agentic workflow where the same context gets extended over and over — that's where SGLang really shines.

Alex: Right. And this is also why it's so good at structured generation. When you're constraining output to a schema, you're effectively exploring a tree of valid continuations. RadixAttention's tree structure maps naturally onto that problem.

Jenny: Okay, so three engines, three philosophies. Where does Dynamo fit? You said there were four.

Alex: Dynamo is fundamentally different from the other three. It's not really an inference engine in the same sense — it's an orchestration framework. NVIDIA open-sourced it in early 2025, and its philosophy is disaggregation-first. It doesn't replace vLLM or TensorRT-LLM — it sits above them and orchestrates them.

Jenny: Wait, so it's like... Kubernetes for inference engines?

Alex: Close. Think of it more like a control plane that can split the inference pipeline into stages. The big idea is disaggregated inference — separating the prefill phase from the decode phase and running them on different hardware.

Jenny: Why would you want to do that? They're two steps of the same request.

Alex: Because they have completely different compute profiles. Prefill is compute-bound — you're processing the entire input prompt in one big matrix multiplication. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound — you're generating one token at a time, reading the whole KV cache each step. Imagine running your batch processing and your real-time API serving on the same EC2 instances. You could, but at fifty-plus GPU scale, it makes sense to have pools specialized for each workload.

Jenny: Oh, so prefill workers are like your compute-optimized instances and decode workers are like your memory-optimized instances. Different hardware profiles for different phases of the same request.

Alex: Exactly. And Dynamo's Rust-based control plane handles routing requests between them, managing the KV cache transfer, all of that. But — and this is important — you only need this at scale. If you're running eight GPUs, the overhead of disaggregation isn't worth it. This is a fifty-plus GPU architecture.

Jenny: And you need high-bandwidth interconnect between the prefill and decode pools, right? Because you're shipping KV cache state between them.

Alex: You do. NVLink, InfiniBand, that kind of thing. Without it, the transfer latency kills your time-to-first-token advantage.

Jenny: So let me see if I can summarize the decision tree. Start with vLLM — it's on-demand, it works, broadest support. If you're on NVIDIA and you've proven your workload and you have the MLOps team, look at TensorRT-LLM for that ten to thirty percent throughput bump. If structured generation or heavy prefix sharing is your bottleneck, SGLang might beat both of them. And Dynamo is for when you're at true fleet scale and need to disaggregate prefill from decode.

Alex: That's exactly right. And I'd add one more thing — don't pick based on benchmark numbers alone. Just like you wouldn't pick an EC2 instance type based solely on spec sheets. You run your actual workload, with your actual prompt distributions, at your actual concurrency levels.

Jenny: Because a benchmark with fixed-length inputs and outputs tells you about as much as a synthetic CPU benchmark tells you about your real application performance.

Alex: Precisely. The real performance depends on your mix of short and long prompts, your batching patterns, whether you're doing multi-turn or single-shot, whether you need structured output — all of it.

Jenny: Okay, this is a great foundation. So looking ahead — we've got the engines, we understand the tradeoffs. What comes next?
