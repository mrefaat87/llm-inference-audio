Alex: So Jenny, I want to start with a problem you've actually lived through at AWS. Imagine you're running a SaaS platform, and every customer wants their own custom-tuned version of your service. Like, not just config — actually different behavior.

Jenny: Right, and the naive answer is you spin up a separate fleet per customer. Which is fine until you have, what, fifty customers? Then your capacity planning is a nightmare and your utilization tanks.

Alex: Exactly. Now translate that to LLMs. Every customer wants a fine-tuned model. The naive version is full fine-tuning — you take a 70B base model and you update every single parameter on their data.

Jenny: And in BF16 that's, what, 140 gigs of weights?

Alex: 140 gigs of weights, plus optimizer state on top of that during training, plus you end up with a 140-gig checkpoint per customer. Now imagine storing that for every tenant. It's operationally insane.

Jenny: Okay so that's the "dedicated fleet per customer" version. Expensive, doesn't scale, capacity is fragmented. What's the alternative?

Alex: The alternative is LoRA. Low-Rank Adaptation. And the core idea is beautiful — instead of updating the whole weight matrix W, you freeze W entirely. You don't touch it. Then you train a tiny additive patch on the side, ΔW, and you decompose that patch into two skinny matrices, A and B.

Jenny: Skinny how?

Alex: So if W is d-by-k — big square-ish matrix — A is d-by-r and B is r-by-k, where r is tiny. Like 8, 16, maybe 64.

Jenny: Wait. So instead of training d times k parameters, you're training d times r plus r times k. And r is, like, two orders of magnitude smaller than d.

Alex: Right. The whole adapter ends up being something like 0.1 to 1 percent of the base model. Tens of megabytes instead of hundreds of gigs. Training drops from weeks to hours.

Jenny: Hold on though. This feels too good. Why does this even work? You're claiming a tiny patch captures what full fine-tuning captures. There has to be a catch.

Alex: That's the right question. The empirical observation behind LoRA is that when you fine-tune a pretrained model, the change in the weights — that ΔW — has low intrinsic rank. Meaning, you're not really teaching the model new directions. You're nudging it along a low-dimensional subspace of behaviors it already knows.

Jenny: Oh. So the base model already contains the capability, fine-tuning is just... steering it.

Alex: Exactly. It's not learning to speak French from scratch. It's learning "be more formal," or "follow this customer's product schema," or "use this support tone." Those are nudges. A rank-16 adapter captures most of what you need for typical task adaptation.

Jenny: And what if it's not a nudge? What if I genuinely need new capability?

Alex: Then you bump the rank up. Or, if it's something really disruptive — a totally new language, a fundamentally different style of reasoning — you go back to full fine-tuning. LoRA isn't magic, it has a regime where it works.

Jenny: Got it. Okay so now let's talk about what this looks like at runtime. Because the math is one thing, but I care about how it actually executes.

Alex: Good. So the forward pass becomes y equals W times x, plus alpha times A times B times x. Mathematically you can group it as y equals (W plus alpha-A-B) times x. One single matrix.

Jenny: So I can just merge them ahead of time and run one matmul?

Alex: You can. And if you only ever serve one adapter on a replica, that's exactly what you should do. Merge it. Zero overhead. Forward pass runs at full base-model speed. The 1 to 3 percent throughput regression from running LoRA unmerged just disappears.

Jenny: But you said "if you only ever serve one." Which implies most of the time you don't.

Alex: Right, this is where it gets interesting. The whole point of LoRA in production is multi-tenancy. One base model in HBM, dozens or hundreds of customer adapters layered on top, and per-request you pick which adapter to apply.

Jenny: Wait wait wait. So this is like — okay let me make sure I have this right. The base 70B is the shared service binary. It's loaded once, lives in HBM. And then each tenant has their own little 50-megabyte config patch that gets layered in at request time.

Alex: That's exactly it. And if you merge, you've baked in one specific tenant. You've lost the ability to swap. Multi-tenant serving has to keep the LoRA branch separate — small extra matmuls per layer, but the base stays neutral.

Jenny: So you pay a small tax for the flexibility of swapping.

Alex: Small, but real. And this is where the operational story gets juicy. Because now think about cost. You've got 140 gigs of base in HBM — call it 35 gigs if you're running INT4 quantized — and then adapters at maybe 50 to 500 megs each, sitting warm in HBM or host RAM.

Jenny: So I can fit, what, a hundred adapters on a single replica?

Alex: Easily. And vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM all support this multi-LoRA mode now. The amortization is incredible — your base model utilization stays high because every tenant's traffic goes through the same weights.

Jenny: Okay so this is where I want to push. Swapping adapters isn't free, right? Like, in cache terms, if a request comes in for an adapter I don't have hot, I have to fault it in.

Alex: Correct. And that's the failure mode. If the adapter is warm in HBM, swap is microseconds. If it's in host RAM, milliseconds. But if it's only on object store — S3, basically — you're looking at a hundred milliseconds plus to fetch it over the network. On the critical path of a request.

Jenny: That'll wreck your P99 instantly.

Alex: It will absolutely wreck your P99. And here's where it ties back to scheduling. You can't just round-robin requests across replicas anymore. Because if tenant A's adapter is warm on replica 1 and cold on replica 2, sending tenant A's request to replica 2 costs you a hundred-millisecond adapter fetch.

Jenny: Oh. Oh, this is exactly tenant-aware routing. Like sticky sessions, but for model weights.

Alex: That's the analogy. They call it LoRA-aware routing. You hash on adapter ID, prefer the replica that already has it loaded, and only spill to other replicas when your preferred one is over some utilization threshold.

Jenny: And without that, what happens?

Alex: Without it, every request potentially loads its adapter from scratch. You get adapter thrash. Replicas spend their time pulling adapters from S3 instead of doing useful work. Per-tenant tail latency explodes. Multi-LoRA serving falls apart.

Jenny: So this is the part that infrastructure people miss. They think LoRA is a model decision. But you're saying it's a coordinated decision across the model, the engine, the scheduler, and the storage tier.

Alex: All four. Pick any one in isolation and the others will not save you. You can have the world's best LoRA training, the best multi-LoRA engine, but if your scheduler is dumb about tenant affinity, you've lost.

Jenny: Hmm. One more thing — I want to come back to QLoRA. You mentioned 4-bit. What's the deal there?

Alex: QLoRA is the operational twist. You keep the base model in 4-bit quantized form — they use a format called NF4 — during both training and inference. Then you train the LoRA adapter in BF16 on top.

Jenny: So the big frozen thing is tiny, and the small trainable thing is full precision.

Alex: Exactly. And the result is dramatic. Training a 70B fine-tune used to mean 8 H100s. With QLoRA, single H100. That's a step-change in accessibility. Quality is essentially indistinguishable from training on the unquantized base. Most modern open-source toolchains — Unsloth, axolotl, PEFT — default to QLoRA now.

Jenny: That's wild. Okay, so to bring this all home — what's next?

Alex: So we've spent this episode on architecture innovations. New attention variants, state-space models, multi-token prediction, distillation, and now LoRA. All of these are about making the model itself cheaper or more flexible. But we've been hinting at something the whole time — scheduling.

Jenny: Right, you keep saying things like "the scheduler has to know about this" or "LoRA-aware routing."

Alex: Because these architectural choices keep pushing complexity into the request layer. Different adapters per request, different cache strategies per request, different speculation strategies per request. The scheduler is no longer a dumb load balancer. It's making semantic decisions about every inbound query.

Jenny: So the next frontier isn't really a model frontier. It's an orchestration frontier.

Alex: That's exactly right. The next thing we need to talk about is what happens when you have ten thousand requests per second hitting a fleet, every one of them with different adapter affinity, different cache state, different latency SLOs. How do you schedule that?

Jenny: That sounds like the hardest distributed systems problem we've talked about yet.

Alex: It is. And it's where everything we've covered — KV cache, prefix cache, LoRA, speculative decoding, disaggregation — comes together into one brutal optimization problem. We'll get into it next time.
