Alex: So I want to start with a question that sounds simple but actually breaks most people's intuition. If you have an H100 sitting there, and you're running inference on it, and you check the utilization — what do you think a really well-tuned system looks like?

Jenny: I mean, my instinct from the EC2 world is, you want it pegged. Ninety percent CPU utilization, you're getting your money's worth. Below fifty, you're wasting capacity.

Alex: Right, that's the cloud infra reflex. So here's the thing that's going to bend your brain a little. A state-of-the-art inference system, running decode, will often show one to five percent MFU. And that can still be optimal.

Jenny: Wait. One to five percent? That sounds like something is catastrophically broken. If I saw that on a fleet dashboard I'd page someone.

Alex: Exactly. And this is why we have to start with the roofline model, because MFU on its own lies to you. The roofline says: every operation a GPU does is either compute-bound or bandwidth-bound. Never both. And which one it is depends on a single number called arithmetic intensity.

Jenny: Okay, define that for me.

Alex: Arithmetic intensity is FLOPs performed divided by bytes moved between HBM — the GPU's main memory — and the chip itself. Units are ops per byte. So for every byte you drag across that memory bus, how much math do you do with it before you throw it away?

Jenny: Got it. So low intensity means I'm shuttling tons of data for very little computation. High intensity means I load once and crunch a lot.

Alex: Exactly. And every GPU has its own break-even ratio — peak FLOPs divided by peak HBM bandwidth. On an H100, in FP16, that's about 295 ops per byte. If your operation is below 295, you hit the bandwidth ceiling first. The arithmetic units literally finish their work and sit idle waiting for data.

Jenny: Hang on. So the GPU can do the math faster than memory can feed it?

Alex: Way faster. Modern GPUs have outrageously more compute than bandwidth. It's like having a Ferrari engine fed by a garden hose. Above 295, you're compute-bound — the data shows up faster than the FLOPs can consume it. The chip's ops:byte ratio is just the breakeven where those two ceilings cross.

Jenny: Okay, so this is basically the GPU's version of, are you CPU-bound or IO-bound. Which... is a question I've been answering since I started in infra.

Alex: That is exactly the right mental model. And now the punchline: decode attention has an arithmetic intensity of about 62 ops per byte.

Jenny: Sixty-two. And the breakeven is 295.

Alex: So decode is squarely on the bandwidth ceiling. Not close to it — way under it. Every decode step, the GPU loads the entire KV cache from HBM, multiplies it by one new query vector, and that's it. You're moving gigabytes to do a tiny amount of math.

Jenny: Okay, but why? Why does it have to load the whole KV cache for one token?

Alex: Because attention is, by definition, the new token looking at every previous token. You can't shortcut that. You have to bring the whole history in to compute what the next token attends to.

Jenny: So the FLOPs are cheap and the data movement is the entire job.

Alex: Right. And now the MFU number makes sense. MFU asks, what fraction of peak FLOPs am I using? But during decode, FLOPs aren't the bottleneck — bandwidth is. So a system running at one percent MFU during decode might be at ninety percent memory bandwidth utilization. Which is the actual binding constraint.

Jenny: Oh. Oh, that's the thing. You're measuring the wrong resource.

Alex: That's the whole insight. It's like measuring CPU utilization on a service that's actually disk-bound. CPU looks idle, you think you have headroom, you pile on more traffic — and everything melts because the disk was already saturated.

Jenny: I have literally lived that incident. Multiple times.

Alex: So in inference, the better metric for decode is MBU — Model Bandwidth Utilization. Actual HBM bandwidth used divided by peak. A well-tuned decode system should hit 70 to 90 percent MBU. That's your "pegged at ninety percent CPU" equivalent.

Jenny: Okay so if MFU is the wrong metric for decode, why does anyone still report MFU?

Alex: Two reasons. One, training is genuinely compute-bound, so MFU is the right metric there — 35 to 50 percent is great, above 50 is excellent. Two, prefill is also compute-bound. When you process a long prompt, the arithmetic intensity is often above 500 ops per byte. You're doing big dense matmuls, the GPU is fed.

Jenny: So prefill and decode aren't just different phases — they're on opposite sides of the roofline.

Alex: That's the first-principles reason chunked prefill exists. That's why disaggregation exists — running prefill on one set of GPUs and decode on another. That's why continuous batching helps. They're all attempts to deal with the fact that one workload is compute-bound and the other is bandwidth-bound, and you can't optimize them with the same knobs.

Jenny: Okay, now I want to ask the obvious question. Which is — if decode is bandwidth-bound, what do you actually do about it? Because just saying "buy a GPU with more bandwidth" feels like the lazy answer.

Alex: It's actually one of the answers. Going from H100 to H200 — same compute, more HBM bandwidth — drops the breakeven from 295 to about 206 ops per byte. Same chip, easier to feed. But the deeper answers all come back to one principle: push the operation's arithmetic intensity above the chip's ops:byte ratio.

Jenny: How?

Alex: Three levers. Move less data per FLOP — that's FlashAttention's tiling, that's GQA and MLA shrinking the KV cache. Or amortize the same data load across more work — that's batching. Bigger batch, same KV load, more queries riding along, intensity goes up. Or change the algorithm so the math-to-bytes ratio fundamentally shifts.

Jenny: That third one is interesting. That sounds like more than an optimization.

Alex: And that's the perfect bridge to the last concept, which is the one I want you to walk away with. Jensen Huang has this idea he uses culturally at NVIDIA called Speed of Light. Before you ask "why is this slow," you ask "what's the theoretical fastest this could possibly go, given physics?" That's SOL. MFU just tells you how close you are to it.

Jenny: So SOL is the unachievable upper bound, and your job is to figure out which part of the gap is physics and which part is engineering inefficiency.

Alex: Exactly. And the thing I love about SOL thinking is it forces honesty. If you're at 40 percent MFU, that's 60 percent gap. Some of that gap is communication overhead — physically necessary, can't eliminate. Some is kernel launch overhead, pipeline bubbles, fragmentation — all attackable. Every optimization in this whole chapter is an attack on a specific source of that gap.

Jenny: Okay, but here's what I want to know. Some of those attacks must be way more impactful than others, right? Like, fixing kernel launch overhead is not the same kind of win as inventing FlashAttention.

Alex: That's the perfect setup for the last idea — unhobblers. The term comes from Leopold Aschenbrenner. An unhobbler isn't a constant-factor speedup. It removes a constraint that was capping the entire scaling curve. Attention itself in 2017 was an unhobbler — RNNs couldn't see distant tokens, so quality was capped no matter how much you scaled. FlashAttention in 2022 was an unhobbler — without it, contexts above 4K were economically infeasible because of the O(n-squared) memory cost. MoE was an unhobbler — it broke the link between parameter count and per-token compute. MLA broke the KV cache memory wall.

Jenny: So the test is — does this make existing things faster, or does it make previously impossible things possible.

Alex: That's the exact question to ask. Quantization, kernel fusion, batching — those are constant-factor wins. Important, real, but they don't change the shape of the curve. Unhobblers change the curve. And when you're evaluating any new technique — at NVIDIA, at AWS, anywhere — that's the lens. Constant factor or curve change?

Jenny: Okay, so let me try to land this. Roofline tells me what kind of work I'm doing. MFU tells me how close I am to the FLOPs ceiling, but only if I'm actually compute-bound — otherwise I should be looking at MBU. SOL tells me what the physics ceiling actually is. And unhobblers are the moves that don't just close the gap to SOL — they raise SOL itself by removing a constraint.

Alex: That's the whole framework. And it's why the rest of this chapter — engines, benchmarking, cost economics — only makes sense once you have this lens. Because the next thing we're going to talk about is choosing inference engines, which is basically choosing between ECS, EKS, and Lambda for inference workloads. Each engine makes different roofline tradeoffs.

Jenny: And I'm guessing the "which engine should I pick" answer is going to depend entirely on whether my workload is prefill-heavy or decode-heavy.

Alex: You're going to be dangerous by the end of this chapter. Because after engines, we get into benchmarking — which is where most people lie to themselves with averages and miss the P99 cliff. And then cost economics, where tokens per second per dollar replaces requests per second as the unit you actually care about.

Jenny: Cost-per-token as the new cost-per-request. That reframes the entire capacity planning conversation.

Alex: It does. And once you've got roofline, MFU versus MBU, SOL, and unhobblers in your head — every one of those next conversations gets sharper. You stop arguing about numbers and start arguing about which ceiling you're hitting and whether it's one you can break.
