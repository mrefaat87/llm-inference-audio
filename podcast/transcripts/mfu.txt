Alex: So Jenny, I want to start today with a question. When you were running EC2 fleets at scale, what was the single number you looked at to know if your fleet was healthy?

Jenny: CPU utilization, obviously. If I'm at 80% average across the fleet, I'm in good shape. If I'm at 20%, I'm wasting money. If I'm at 98%, I'm about to have a bad day.

Alex: Right. Simple, intuitive, actionable. Now, GPUs have an equivalent metric called MFU — Model FLOP Utilization. And here's the thing that's going to bother you: state-of-the-art systems run at 35 to 50 percent MFU, and people celebrate that.

Jenny: Wait, 40% utilization and that's considered good? If I told my VP my EC2 fleet was running at 40% CPU utilization, I'd be asked why I'm not downsizing immediately.

Alex: And that's exactly the right instinct, but here's where GPUs break your mental model. An H100 can theoretically do 494 teraFLOPS of useful math per second in BF16. That's the peak. If your training run is actually achieving 200 teraFLOPS of useful model computation, your MFU is about 40%. The other 60% isn't wasted the way idle CPU cycles are wasted. A lot of it is the GPU doing necessary work that just isn't model math.

Jenny: Okay, unpack that. What's eating the other 60%?

Alex: The biggest one, especially for inference, is the memory bandwidth bottleneck. Think of it this way. Your GPU has these incredibly fast arithmetic units — thousands of cores that can multiply matrices at absurd speed. But those cores need data to work on. The model weights, the activations, the KV cache — all of that lives in HBM, High Bandwidth Memory. And even though HBM is fast by memory standards, it's still way slower than the arithmetic units.

Jenny: So it's like having a thousand workers on an assembly line, but only one conveyor belt feeding them parts.

Alex: That's a perfect analogy. During decode — when the model is generating tokens one at a time — each step only does a tiny amount of math per weight that gets loaded. You load the entire model's weights from memory, do one small matrix-vector multiply, and produce one token. The arithmetic takes microseconds. The memory load takes milliseconds. Your thousand workers are standing around waiting for parts most of the time.

Jenny: Oh, so that's why you said MFU breaks my mental model. The GPU isn't idle in the way a CPU is idle. It's actively bottlenecked on a completely different resource.

Alex: Exactly. And this is where it gets interesting. During decode, a well-optimized system might show 1 to 5 percent MFU. One to five percent! That sounds catastrophic.

Jenny: That sounds like something is on fire.

Alex: But that same system could be running at 90% memory bandwidth utilization. The actual bottleneck resource is nearly maxed out. You're measuring CPU utilization when the real constraint is disk I/O. You're looking at the wrong gauge.

Jenny: So what's the right gauge for inference?

Alex: For decode specifically, it's MBU — Model Bandwidth Utilization. That's actual HBM bandwidth used divided by peak HBM bandwidth. A well-tuned decode system should hit 70 to 90 percent MBU. Now, prefill is different. Prefill processes the entire prompt in one big batch, so it actually is compute-bound, more like training. Prefill can hit 30 to 50 percent MFU. But decode? MBU is your metric.

Jenny: This maps to something I've seen in practice, actually. We had services where CPU looked fine but the EBS volume was completely saturated. We were looking at the wrong metric for months. So for inference, you need at least MBU for decode efficiency, and then I'd imagine something for cost?

Alex: Right. Tokens per second per dollar is your unit economics metric — it's the new cost-per-request. Then TTFT and time between tokens at P50 and P99 for your SLO compliance. And here's one that'll feel very familiar — KV cache utilization percentage.

Jenny: Oh, that's like capacity pool utilization in Auto Scaling. If your KV cache is full, you can't accept new requests even if the GPU compute is sitting there available.

Alex: Exactly like that. Your GPU could be at 5% MFU with plenty of compute headroom, but if KV cache memory is full, new requests get blocked. Just like having CPU headroom but no available instances in your capacity pool.

Jenny: Okay, so MFU tells us where we are. But how do we think about where we could be? Like, what's the theoretical ceiling?

Alex: And that brings us to something Jensen Huang talks about constantly at NVIDIA. He calls it SOL — Speed of Light. Not the physics constant, but the concept. Before you ask "why is this slow," you first ask "what is the theoretical fastest this could possibly be, given the physical constraints of this hardware?"

Jenny: So for a GPU, SOL would be 100% of peak TFLOPS with zero overhead of any kind.

Alex: Right. And MFU is literally your distance from SOL. A 40% MFU means you're at 40% of the speed of light for this system. Now, here's the powerful part. That 60% gap? You can decompose it. Some of it is fundamental physics — communication between GPUs in a tensor parallel setup requires actual data transfer, and that takes real time. You can't optimize that away. But a lot of it is engineering friction. Pipeline bubbles where GPUs sit idle waiting for adjacent stages. Kernel launch overhead — every time you launch a small computation on the GPU, there's fixed setup cost. Memory access patterns that aren't optimal.

Jenny: So SOL thinking basically gives you a framework for triage. You look at the gap between where you are and SOL, and you categorize each piece as either "physics, accept it" or "engineering, attack it."

Alex: And this is apparently cultural at NVIDIA. Not just for senior engineers. Frontline engineers use it naturally. When someone proposes a performance number or a timeline, the immediate response is "what's the SOL here, and why aren't we at it?" It's a first-principles forcing function.

Jenny: I love that. We had something similar with scaling — we'd ask "what's the minimum number of instances that could theoretically handle this load" and then work backward from there. Same energy.

Alex: Same exact energy. And once you have SOL thinking in your head, it naturally leads to a really important question: what if the ceiling itself could be raised?

Jenny: What do you mean, raised? SOL is the theoretical maximum. By definition you can't go past it.

Alex: You can't go past it for a given architecture. But what if someone invents a new architecture that has a completely different, higher ceiling? That's the concept of an unhobbler.

Jenny: Unhobbler. That's a vivid term.

Alex: It comes from Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness essay. An unhobbler is a discovery that doesn't just make existing things faster by some constant factor — it removes a constraint that was capping the entire scaling curve. It changes the shape of what's possible.

Jenny: Give me a concrete example.

Alex: The most dramatic one is the attention mechanism from the original Transformer paper in 2017. Before that, we had RNNs — recurrent neural networks. RNNs processed sequences one step at a time, and information about early tokens would degrade as the sequence got longer. No matter how big you made an RNN, it couldn't reliably attend to something that happened a thousand tokens ago. The quality was fundamentally capped.

Jenny: So it's not that RNNs were slow. They were hitting a hard ceiling on what they could learn, period.

Alex: Exactly. Attention removed that ceiling entirely. Suddenly every token could directly attend to every other token regardless of distance. It didn't make RNNs 10% better. It created an entirely new paradigm. Modern LLMs, everything we're doing with inference infrastructure — that's the unlock.

Jenny: Okay so that's the big paradigm one. What about something closer to the infrastructure layer?

Alex: FlashAttention in 2022. Standard attention has O of n-squared memory cost — quadratic in sequence length. At 4,000 tokens, it was manageable. At 32,000 tokens, you're spending more time reading the attention matrix from HBM than doing actual math. Contexts beyond about 4k tokens were economically infeasible.

Jenny: So the SOL for long-context inference was capped by a memory access pattern, not by compute.

Alex: Right. And FlashAttention didn't change the math of attention. It changed the memory access pattern. By tiling the computation to stay in the GPU's fast SRAM instead of constantly round-tripping to HBM, it slashed the memory bandwidth requirement. That single change unlocked the entire long-context era. 4k to 32k to 128k to a million tokens. Not a 10% speedup — a new capability that didn't exist before.

Jenny: That's like the jump from spinning disks to SSDs. You didn't make the disk faster — you removed the seek time constraint entirely, and suddenly entire application architectures became possible that weren't before.

Alex: That's a great analogy. And there are more. MoE — Mixture of Experts — unhobbled the coupling between parameter count and compute cost. Before MoE, every parameter activated on every token. Want more knowledge? Pay for more compute on every single inference. MoE breaks that link. Only a fraction of experts activate per token, so you get massive knowledge capacity at practical compute cost.

Jenny: So models like GPT-4 and DeepSeek can have huge parameter counts without the inference cost scaling linearly with parameters.

Alex: Exactly. And then there's MLA — Multi-head Latent Attention — which attacked the KV cache memory wall. Remember how we talked about KV cache being like your capacity pool? MLA dramatically compresses the KV cache, which means you can serve much longer contexts without running out of that pool.

Jenny: So the pattern I'm seeing is — optimization techniques like quantization or kernel fusion, those are like tuning your Auto Scaling policies. You're getting more out of the same infrastructure. But unhobblers are like when AWS launched Graviton or spot instances. It's not making the existing thing better. It's changing the economics entirely.

Alex: That's exactly the right framework. And here's the question to carry with you: when you evaluate any new technique — whether it's a new serving optimization, a new architecture, a new hardware feature — ask yourself, is this a constant-factor improvement, or does it change the scaling curve? That tells you how transformative it's likely to be.

Jenny: So where does all of this lead us? We've got MFU telling us where we are, SOL telling us where we could be, and unhobblers telling us the ceiling can move. What comes next?

Alex: Well, now that we have the vocabulary for measuring and reasoning about GPU efficiency, the next step is applying it. We're going to look at the actual optimization techniques — the engineering attacks on that gap between real performance and SOL. Things like speculative decoding, which tries to unhobble the one-token-at-a-time sequential ceiling. Disaggregated inference, where you separate prefill and decode onto different hardware because they have completely different SOL profiles.

Jenny: Because prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bandwidth-bound. You just told me they need different metrics — so it makes sense they'd want different hardware.

Alex: Exactly. And the question stops being "how do I make my GPU faster" and becomes "how do I get each part of the system closer to its own SOL, and which constraints am I willing to invest in unhobbling?" That's where inference infrastructure gets really interesting.

Jenny: I love it. We went from "why is my GPU at 40% utilization" to "maybe 40% is the wrong number to look at" to "maybe the ceiling itself can be moved." That's a good arc. Alright, next time — the actual attacks on the SOL gap. Can't wait.
