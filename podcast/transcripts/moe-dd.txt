Alex: So I want to start with a number that messed with my head when I first saw it. DeepSeek-V3 is 671 billion parameters total, but only 37 billion of those are active per token. And the question I had — and I think anyone coming from the vLLM world has — is, okay, fine, that's the model. But what does that actually do to the serving system?

Jenny: Right, because vLLM trained us to think a certain way. Continuous batching, PagedAttention, prefix caching — those all assume every token marches through the same set of weights. The compute graph is the same shape every time.

Alex: Exactly. Dense models give you this beautiful property that the path through the network is identical for every token. Every weight gets touched. Your per-token compute budget is a constant. The whole vLLM architecture — the scheduler, the KV manager, the kernels — is built on top of that constant.

Jenny: And MoE just blows that up.

Alex: It blows it up in a really specific way. Parameters and compute are decoupled. You have 671 billion parameters sitting in memory, but per token you only do 37 billion worth of work. Which sounds like a win — and it is, for compute. But now ask yourself, where do those 671 billion parameters live?

Jenny: On a lot of GPUs. Even at FP8 that's, what, around 700 GB?

Alex: About 700 GB of weights. Too big for a single H100 node. Too big for a single H200 node. The DeepSeek tech report tells you their actual deployed topology — four nodes, thirty-two GPUs for prefill, and forty nodes, three hundred and twenty GPUs for decode. There is no single-machine option for a frontier MoE. Multi-node aggregation is the starting condition, before any optimization happens.

Jenny: Wait, hold on. Forty nodes for decode? That feels backwards. I'd expect prefill — which is the wide compute sweep over a long input — to need more GPUs than decode, where you're generating one token at a time.

Alex: Yeah, it's a great instinct and it's wrong, and the reason it's wrong is the whole story. Decode for MoE is memory-bandwidth-bound across hundreds of experts, not compute-bound. You need the experts spread out across more GPUs precisely because each token only activates a few of them and you want each GPU to hold fewer experts so you have headroom for the KV cache and a wider per-expert batch. We'll come back to that.

Jenny: Okay, I'll let you build to it. So pain point one is just, the parameters don't fit anywhere small.

Alex: Pain point one. Pain point two is the one that actually defines the systems work. When you spread experts across GPUs — that's expert parallelism, EP — every MoE layer becomes an all-to-all communication. The Aurora paper draws this out really cleanly. A layer is gate, then first all-to-all to dispatch tokens to whichever GPU owns the expert they got routed to, then the FFN computation on that expert, then a second all-to-all to combine results back, then aggregation. Two all-to-alls per MoE layer.

Jenny: Per layer. And how many MoE layers does DeepSeek have?

Alex: Three dense layers and fifty-eight MoE layers in V3. That's a hundred and sixteen all-to-alls on the forward pass. Per token. Per request.

Jenny: That is just a brutal amount of network shuffle. And I want to make sure I'm seeing this right — this is map-reduce, basically. The shuffle phase of map-reduce, but you're paying for it twice every layer.

Alex: That's the analogy I keep coming back to. Map-reduce shuffle, but synchronous, two-phase, and the per-rank payload sizes change every layer because routing is dynamic. Which gets us to pain point three. The gate function decides where each token goes, and the gate output is a function of the token. So the routing table is recomputed every layer, every token. You cannot pre-allocate based on a static plan. Aurora puts it really plainly — the dynamic selection of experts results in uneven distribution of experts among GPUs, so some GPUs are heavily loaded while others are idle.

Jenny: So now I'm thinking about this like load balancing. In a stateless web tier, you have a load balancer that hashes requests across replicas. If your hash function is bad you get a hot shard. But you can usually look at the traffic over time and rebalance.

Alex: You can. And that's exactly what production MoE systems do — we'll get to EPLB. But the key thing here is, the imbalance regenerates itself every single layer, because the routing key isn't a stable session ID, it's a learned function of intermediate hidden states. Every transformer layer is a fresh load-balancing problem.

Jenny: Okay so that's the setup. Multi-node by default, all-to-all dominant, dynamic routing creating fresh load imbalance every layer. So when you say MoE is a systems problem, you don't mean it's hard kernels. You mean it's hard distributed systems.

Alex: Exactly. The kernels matter — TRT-LLM has done beautiful kernel surgery on MLA and on the all-to-all itself — but the dominant cost isn't a kernel. The dominant cost is network shuffle inside a synchronous collective. Which leads me into segment two, because I want to pin down just how dominant.

Jenny: Yeah let's do it. How bad is the all-to-all actually?

Alex: It depends on the cluster shape and the model, but the cited numbers are striking. Aurora references an internal study where all-to-all consumes over sixty percent of inference time on a four-GPU setup, and that overhead grows with cluster size. Occult — which is the ICML 2025 paper from UNC and UCLA — says the figure is "over forty percent of runtime in large-scale training" for modern MoE LLMs.

Jenny: Forty to sixty percent. So your GPUs are spending half their time waiting on the network, basically.

Alex: Half their time inside the collective. Not literally on the wire, but inside the collective, which because it's synchronous includes idle time. And that's the thing I want to drill into. Let me explain why two all-to-alls per layer, and not one. Aurora is explicit about this. Two communications occur in opposite data flows. The first dispatches tokens to the GPU that owns the chosen expert. The expert runs. Then the second exchanges expert outputs back so the original sequence is reassembled before the next layer's attention starts. The data sizes match. What dispatched must combine.

Jenny: Right, because you can't run attention on a reordered sequence. Attention needs the original positional order back, plus all of its KV neighbors in the right place.

Alex: Exactly. And there are three structural reasons all-to-all here is worse than a generic shuffle. Aurora isolates them, and I think they're worth saying carefully. One: synchronous all-to-all. Computation can only begin once every GPU has completed data transmission. So the slowest sender pins everyone.

Jenny: That's a global barrier.

Alex: It's a global barrier. Two: reversed all-to-all communications. Both directions move the same volume per pair, and there's no way to batch or amortize across the two phases. You can't piggyback, because the second phase depends on the FFN result of the first. And three: communication and computation don't overlap. Each step starts after the previous step ends.

Jenny: Wait, the non-overlap is interesting because in normal pipelined systems you can hide latency. Like, you do compute on stage N while you're already prefetching for stage N+1. That's CPU pipelining. That's Spark stages. That's how everything in distributed systems works.

Alex: Yes. And in vanilla MoE, you can't, because the dependency chain is gate to dispatch to FFN to combine to aggregate to next layer's attention. Every arrow is a hard dependency. There's no slack to slide compute under communication.

Jenny: Okay that's already painful. Now layer dynamic routing on top.

Alex: Layer dynamic routing on top. Token volume per pair — how many tokens GPU four sends to GPU seven on layer twenty-three — is per-batch data. The gate decides it for that layer, for that batch. So your all-to-all is synchronous, two-phased, non-overlapping with compute, and has unpredictable per-pair payload sizes. The synchronous-blocking nature is what turns dynamic skew into idle time. The slowest expert pins the entire collective.

Jenny: Hot shard problem. Some expert gets popular for a particular batch, that GPU is over-subscribed, everyone else waits.

Alex: Hot shard problem, but — and this is the wrinkle — recreated every layer.

Jenny: Right. Because the gate's decision is fresh.

Alex: The gate's decision is fresh. And then there's the other knob nobody likes thinking about — heterogeneous clusters. Aurora calls this out specifically. Production GPU fleets accumulate generations over time. You have H100s, you have A100s still running, you have H200s coming online. Aurora says GPU heterogeneity, common due to incremental deployments, adds further complexity to MoE deployment. A faster GPU finishing its experts early gains nothing if it has to wait for a slower peer in the synchronous all-to-all. There's an Alibaba GPU cluster study Aurora cites that found less than ten percent of GPUs reach eighty percent utilization.

Jenny: Less than ten percent. That is — that's a fleet utilization problem. As an EM I'm staring at a capacity bill and I'm seeing ten cents on the dollar of GPU work.

Alex: That's the picture. And once you've internalized it — synchronous, two-phase, non-overlapping, dynamic, heterogeneous — you can read the entire research literature on MoE inference as a direct attack on one or more of those properties. Which is segment three. Four papers, four different angles on the same bottleneck.

Jenny: Okay so let's walk them. Where do you want to start?

Alex: Let's go in chronological-ish order, because the progression actually matters. ExFlow first. That's Yao et al., out of Ohio State, the MVAPICH lineage. The core insight in ExFlow is that pre-trained MoEs have hidden inter-layer expert affinity. Tokens that get routed to expert i in layer L tend to get routed to a small set of experts in layer L+1. It's a static property of the trained weights, not a dynamic property of the routing.

Jenny: So tokens kind of follow learned highway lanes between layers.

Alex: They do. And because the affinity is static, you can plan placement and routing such that you only need one all-to-all per layer instead of two. They call it context-coherent expert parallelism. The numbers from the paper — up to sixty-seven percent reduction in cross-GPU routing latency, up to two-point-two-times improvement in inference throughput, no fine-tuning, no accuracy loss.

Jenny: Wait. No fine-tuning. So this is a drop-in optimization on existing checkpoints.

Alex: That's the qualifier that makes ExFlow deployable rather than research-only. It's a pure scheduling and placement change on top of the trained weights. You can ship this on top of an existing model.

Jenny: Okay so ExFlow attacks property number — basically the two-all-to-alls property. Cuts it to one when affinity allows.

Alex: Right. ScMoE is the next move. Cai et al. ScMoE's argument is, if the architecture itself doesn't allow comm-compute overlap, change the architecture. Add a shortcut connection that breaks the strict compute-then-communicate-then-compute dependency. The next computation can start as soon as partial communication completes.

Jenny: That's PDL-flavored. Programmatic dependent launch in the kernel world — kernel B starts when A has produced enough output.

Alex: Same family of idea, at a higher level of the stack. ScMoE reports one-point-four-nine-times training speedup and one-point-eight-two-times inference speedup against a top-2 MoE baseline. The cost is this is now a different architecture. You can't drop it on a DeepSeek checkpoint. You have to train from scratch, or at minimum continue-pretrain.

Jenny: So it's a more fundamental change but it solves a more fundamental problem.

Alex: It solves the non-overlap property, yeah. And here's the link forward — DeepSeek's DualPipe pipeline scheduler does something architecturally similar but inside a fixed model. The lineage is direct. ScMoE proved the overlap was achievable; DualPipe achieves it via scheduling within the existing graph.

Jenny: Okay. Aurora next.

Alex: Aurora. Li et al., MPI-SWS, October 2024. This is the most theoretically rigorous of the four, and to me the most production-realistic. The claim is that you should solve two problems jointly that everybody else has solved separately. One, where does each expert live? Two, in the all-to-all, in what order does each GPU send its tokens to other GPUs? Place experts and order transmissions in one optimization.

Jenny: Joint scheduling. That's classic systems territory. And what does ordering buy you?

Alex: It buys you bandwidth contention reduction. If GPU one and GPU two both want to send heavy payloads to GPU three at the same time, you've created a hotspot at GPU three's NIC. If you order sends, you can spread the contention out over the collective. Aurora also has this beautiful trick — colocate experts from different models on the same device. So if you're running multiple MoEs in your fleet, the same device can serve experts from different models, and the synchronous-blocking idle time of model A's collective gets used by model B's compute.

Jenny: Oh, that's bin packing. That's exactly EC2 bin packing across workloads. You have a fast peer that finished its part of model A's collective — instead of letting it idle, it can do work on model B.

Alex: That's the analogy. And the paper analyzes four cases — exclusive vs colocated experts, homogeneous vs heterogeneous GPUs. They give optimal solutions for three of them. The fourth case, colocating experts on heterogeneous GPUs, is NP-hard. They give a polynomial-time approximation that's within one-point-oh-seven of optimal.

Jenny: That's a really tight approximation ratio.

Alex: It is. The headline numbers — up to two-point-three-eight times speedup in homogeneous clusters, up to three-point-five-four times in heterogeneous environments. GPU utilization up to one-point-five times. The heterogeneous number is the production-realistic one because no real fleet is homogeneous.

Jenny: So Aurora attacks the synchronous-blocking property and the heterogeneity property at the same time.

Alex: Both at the same time. And then Occult. This one is Luo, Li, Peng et al. — UNC, UCLA, Minnesota, CUHK, ICML 2025. Occult is the most fundamental rethink of the four. The argument is, the unit of communication shouldn't be the token-to-expert message. It should be the collaboration — pairs of experts co-activated by one token.

Jenny: Wait, so if a token goes to two experts — top-2 routing — those two experts are a collaboration?

Alex: Those two experts are a collaboration. And Occult splits collaborations into intra and inter. Intra-collaboration means the co-activated pair sits on the same device. Communication is local. Effectively free. Inter-collaboration means the experts sit on different devices and communication crosses the network. Optimization target — maximize intra, minimize inter, by choosing where experts live and how tokens route.

Jenny: That's just data locality, but at a different unit of analysis. You're co-locating the things that get accessed together.

Alex: Yes. And they formalize a metric, C_T, the per-token average number of replicas required. Classical MoE has C_T equal to 2 — every token, two experts, two device touches. Occult without pruning gets to one-point-five. Occult with collaborative pruning hits the theoretical minimum of one. They show C_T correlates linearly with wall-clock runtime, which is a really clean empirical result.

Jenny: And the speedup?

Alex: More than one-point-five times across multiple tasks and models, with comparable or superior quality to standard fine-tuning. The code is open at github dot com slash UNITES-Lab slash Occult.

Jenny: Okay. So the four papers as a sequence. ExFlow says, exploit a static property to send half the messages. ScMoE says, change the architecture to hide the messages. Aurora says, schedule the messages to remove the synchronous tail. Occult says, change what counts as a message.

Alex: That's exactly the progression. And production stacks borrow from all four. DualPipe from ScMoE's overlap idea. EPLB has Aurora flavor. ExFlow's expert-affinity awareness shows up in placement decisions. None of these techniques is a clean win on its own — production wins come from stacking them.

Jenny: Okay so let's actually go to production. DeepSeek-V3 is the most-documented frontier MoE deployment in the public record, right?

Alex: It is. And the reason the technical report is so valuable is it tells you both training-side and inference-side parallelism, which most labs don't disclose. So let me start with the model. DeepSeek-V3 is 671 billion total, 37 billion active, trained on 14.8 trillion tokens, total cost 2.788 million H800 GPU hours. No loss spikes, no rollbacks across the entire training run.

Jenny: That's a remarkably stable training run for a model that big.

Alex: It is. Architecturally — Multi-head Latent Attention, DeepSeekMoE, auxiliary-loss-free load balancing, and a Multi-Token Prediction training objective. Training-time parallelism is sixteen-way pipeline parallelism, sixty-four-way expert parallelism across eight nodes, ZeRO-1 data parallelism for the rest. No tensor parallelism during training.

Jenny: Wait. No TP at training? That feels weird — TP is the standard answer to "you have a layer that doesn't fit on one GPU."

Alex: That's the part I want to pause on. The reason they avoided TP at training is memory-bandwidth utilization. TP shards a matmul across GPUs and forces an all-reduce inside every layer. NVLink bandwidth was their bottleneck during training, and TP would have stolen from it. So they used PP plus EP plus ZeRO and engineered around TP entirely.

Jenny: But then at inference they do use TP.

Alex: They do use TP at inference. The deployed prefill is TP4 with sequence parallelism plus DP8 plus EP32. The deployed decode is TP4 plus SP plus DP80 plus EP320. So same model, different parallelism, depending on phase. Training optimizes for end-to-end throughput where memory bandwidth is dominant. Inference optimizes for latency, especially decode, where TP shrinks per-token wall-clock at the cost of bandwidth.

Jenny: So the parallelism strategy isn't a property of the model. It's a property of the workload phase.

Alex: That's the right way to think about it. And it's one of the things that surprised me most in the report. Now — DualPipe. DeepSeek's pipeline-parallelism algorithm overlaps computation and communication within a pair of forward and backward chunks. It reduces pipeline bubbles compared to 1F1B and ZeroBubble, the two prior standards. The conceptual link to ScMoE is direct — ScMoE got overlap by changing the architecture; DualPipe gets it by scheduling within a fixed architecture.

Jenny: How do they pull that off without an architectural change?

Alex: By being very careful about which micro-batches are in flight, and which direction they're moving. The bidirectional pipeline lets attention compute on micro-batch i overlap with MoE all-to-all on micro-batch j and MoE FFN on micro-batch k, all in the same time slice. It's like hyperthreading hiding memory stalls — the overlap comes from having multiple independent units of work resident simultaneously.

Jenny: Okay that makes sense. Now, the all-to-all kernel itself — DeepEP.

Alex: DeepEP is the custom all-to-all kernel. It's tuned for the H800 cluster topology — eight GPUs per node connected by NVLink and NVSwitch, cross-node by InfiniBand. The strategy allocates twenty SMs to cross-node communication, routes each token to at most four nodes, and exploits the bandwidth difference between layers — NVLink at 160 GB per second, IB at 50 GB per second.

Jenny: So three-point-something to one. NVLink is the fast lane.

Alex: And DeepEP fully overlaps the IB and NVLink transfers so the slower fabric is hidden behind the faster one. It's a memory-hierarchy mindset applied to the network — you have a fast tier and a slow tier, you arrange transfers so the slow tier never blocks.

Jenny: That is exactly how you'd organize DRAM and HBM. Or how you'd organize page cache and SSD.

Alex: Same playbook, applied to the network. Now — MLA. Multi-head Latent Attention. This is the reason DeepSeek can fit context this large at this throughput. Standard attention stores per-head K and V tensors in cache — that's a lot of memory per token. MLA compresses K and V jointly into a small latent vector, plus a decoupled key, and stores only those. Reads do a tiny up-projection back to per-head K and V on the fly.

Jenny: So it's a compression trick on the KV cache.

Alex: Yeah. Roughly ten times smaller KV cache, at the cost of an up-projection on every read. The TRT-LLM blog on B200 has an interesting note here — in their MLA implementation, the attention modules use BF16, not FP8, because low-precision attention computation doesn't help with performance. The FLOP count is too small to benefit from lower precision; you're memory-bandwidth bound.

Jenny: Interesting. So precision is a per-kernel decision, not a model-wide one.

Alex: Per-kernel decision driven by what's bottlenecking that kernel. Now, auxiliary-loss-free load balancing — this is a quietly elegant idea. The standard MoE balance trick is to add an auxiliary loss term that penalizes uneven expert utilization. It works, but it hurts model quality, because you're now training against two objectives. DeepSeek replaces it with a learned routing bias. You add a per-expert bias term to the gate output, and you adjust the bias online during training based on observed utilization. No extra loss term, balanced experts, clean quality.

Jenny: That's a neat hack. It's the same idea as exponential weighted moving averages in load shedding — adjust the routing weights based on observed traffic, no extra objective function.

Alex: Same family of trick. And then MTP — multi-token prediction. At training time, the model is trained to predict not just the next token but multiple future tokens via stacked MTP heads. At inference time, those MTP heads can be repurposed as a built-in speculative drafter. TRT-LLM stacks one to three MTP layers in an autoregressive design and reports a 2.82 times acceptance rate for speculative decoding on DeepSeek-R1 on B200.

Jenny: 2.82× acceptance is huge. That's a real win.

Alex: It's one of the larger contributions to their min-latency stack on B200. Now, the inference deployment topology. Two stages, very different shapes. Prefill is four nodes, thirty-two GPUs, TP4 plus SP plus DP8 plus EP32, with thirty-two redundant experts deployed. Decode is forty nodes, three hundred and twenty GPUs, TP4 plus SP plus DP80 plus EP320.

Jenny: Wait, walk me through that EP rank again. Prefill is EP32. Decode is EP320. Ten times wider on decode.

Alex: Ten times wider on decode. And here's the why. Prefill is a wide compute sweep — you're processing thousands of tokens of context at once, attention is computing over all of them, the FFN is hammered hard, broad expert coverage. Compute is the bottleneck. So you concentrate experts on fewer GPUs and let each GPU handle a wider batch.

Jenny: And decode?

Alex: Decode is per-token, autoregressive. One token at a time per request, but many concurrent requests in the batch. Memory bandwidth is the bottleneck — you're reading expert weights and KV cache to process each token. So you spread experts across more GPUs, each GPU holds fewer experts, more KV cache headroom per GPU, wider effective per-expert batch.

Jenny: Right. Okay, that's the answer to my earlier question — it's why decode wants more GPUs.

Alex: That's the answer. And the redundant experts. Thirty-two of them at prefill. These are duplicates of popular experts. Same idea as read replicas for hot keys in a sharded database. Dispatch step has options — if expert seventeen is hot this batch, there are multiple GPUs holding expert seventeen and you can split the load.

Jenny: So this is the production answer to dynamic skew. Don't try to make routing deterministic. Just make popular experts cheap to replicate.

Alex: Exactly. And — this is a big one — DeepSeek does not drop tokens at inference. At training time, MoE systems often use a "capacity factor" that drops overflow tokens routed to over-full experts, to keep per-expert compute predictable. At inference, no dropping. Every token goes to its assigned experts.

Jenny: Why no dropping? I mean, dropping would solve the synchronous-blocking problem, kind of.

Alex: It would solve it by destroying quality. Dropping a token at inference means that token's representation doesn't get the contribution from its assigned expert. Quality degrades. So the production decision is, no dropping — which forces you to use redundant experts and dynamic balancing instead. The thirty-two redundant experts at prefill exist because of the no-dropping policy.

Jenny: Got it. Engineering decisions cascading from a quality decision.

Alex: That's how the whole stack is shaped. And the README — the deepseek-ai DeepSeek-V3 GitHub README — lists six recommended inference frameworks. SGLang, LMDeploy, TensorRT-LLM, vLLM, LightLLM, and DeepSeek-Infer Demo. SGLang and TensorRT-LLM both ship FP8 native weight support. FP8 is the native checkpoint format. BF16 conversion is available.

Jenny: Six frameworks. So the framework choice is itself part of the deployment matrix.

Alex: It is. Different frameworks make different tradeoffs. SGLang is great for prefix-heavy and structured workloads. TRT-LLM is the throughput peak when you can pay the AOT compile cost. vLLM is the general-purpose option. The framework choice is one cell in the deployment matrix, alongside hardware, precision, parallelism, topology.

Jenny: Okay so segment four was DeepSeek's actual production stack. What's NVIDIA doing on top of all this?

Alex: NVIDIA's answer is, what if the rack is the unit of deployment? GB200 NVL72. Seventy-two GPUs in one coherent NVLink domain. The dev blog calls out 130 TB per second of aggregate bandwidth across the rack.

Jenny: 130 TB per second.

Alex: 130 TB per second across the seventy-two-GPU domain. That bandwidth is what makes wide-EP actually work. At EP32, every all-to-all crosses thirty-two GPUs. The cross-rank traffic would otherwise dominate. NVL72's NVLink-everywhere fabric collapses the cross-node penalty down to NVLink speed for everything inside the rack. There's no IB hop inside an NVL72.

Jenny: So the network heterogeneity that DeepSeek's DeepEP had to engineer around — NVLink intra-node, IB inter-node — that goes away on NVL72 for any deployment that fits inside one rack.

Alex: It goes away inside the rack. Across racks, you still have the heterogeneity. But for a single 72-GPU deployment, it's NVLink everywhere. And the headline result from the dev blog is large-scale EP rank 32 delivers up to 1.8 times higher output token throughput per GPU compared to EP rank 8, at 100 tokens per second per user.

Jenny: Per-GPU throughput. That's the right unit.

Alex: That's the right unit. Because the question isn't, can I push more tokens by adding more GPUs — that's almost trivially yes. The question is, do my GPUs each become more productive when I widen EP? And the answer is yes, by 1.8 times. The dev blog gives three concrete reasons. One — less weight-loading pressure per GPU. Two — easier weight reuse by the GroupGEMM kernel, higher arithmetic intensity, more FLOPs per byte of weight loaded. Three — more memory headroom for KV cache per GPU because experts take less.

Jenny: So everywhere I'm relieving pressure — weights, kernel arithmetic intensity, KV cache — and that lets each GPU do more useful work per unit time.

Alex: That's the picture. And then EPLB. The expert-parallel load balancer. Two modes — static for warmup and benchmarking, online for adapting to real traffic. The dev blog frames wide-EP plus disaggregated serving as the two key pillars of their MoE production stack.

Jenny: EPLB is doing what for the static case versus the online case?

Alex: Static placement is a one-time decision based on offline profiling — given this set of experts and this expected traffic distribution, here's how to spread experts across GPUs to balance load. Online is, at runtime, observe which experts are hot, periodically remap.

Jenny: That's just hot-shard rebalancing. Cassandra does it, DynamoDB does it. Consistent hashing with replica adjustment.

Alex: It's the same primitive. And the LMSYS folks who reproduced this on H100s — we'll get to them in a minute — flag a really important caveat. EPLB was tuned on in-distribution data. Production traffic is not in-distribution. So EPLB is a starting point, not a finished product.

Jenny: Yeah, anyone who's run a production load balancer on real traffic knows that.

Alex: Then there's the kernel side. The dev blog mentions custom NCCL kernels for non-static data sizes across large-scale EP. The standard NCCL all-to-all is built around static payload sizes, which is exactly the wrong fit for MoE where per-pair payloads are dynamic. So they wrote custom kernels. The TRT-LLM Part 3 blog goes deeper — FP4 weight-only GEMM quantization for attention output layers, low-precision NVFP4 AlltoAll communication during the combine phase, FP8 context FMHA, MTP LM-head tensor parallelism, separate-qkv format that eliminates Q/K/V concat copies in the context phase.

Jenny: NVFP4 on the AlltoAll. So they're quantizing the wire format.

Alex: They're quantizing the wire format on the combine phase. Which is the analog of compressing payloads before a network shuffle in a distributed sort. Smaller bytes-on-the-wire means more effective bandwidth, less time inside the synchronous collective. The doc-home blog index also lists two forward-looking pieces — one-sided AlltoAll over NVLink, and DWDP, distributed weight data parallelism for high-performance LLM inference on NVL72. The cited sources don't give us deep implementation detail beyond the titles, but the framing — one-sided semantics over NVLink, where the receiver doesn't stall waiting for a matched send — is RDMA-style one-sided put-get versus MPI Send-Recv.

Jenny: So you remove the rendezvous step that synchronous send-receive forces.

Alex: That's the conceptual move. If a deeper claim is needed in interview prep, attribute these as listed-but-not-yet-fully-detailed in the public sources.

Jenny: Got it. And the actual end-to-end speedup story?

Jenny: 5.5 times in one product cycle. That's a lot of compounding optimizations.

Prefill-decode disaggregation, large-scale EP. The numbers they got are the most credible third-party data point we have for DeepSeek-class MoE on commodity Hopper hardware.

Jenny: What did they hit?

Alex: 52.3 thousand input tokens per second per node on prefill. 22.3 thousand output tokens per second per node on decode. For two-thousand-token input sequences. Prefill is four nodes at EP32 — 57,674 tokens per second at one-thousand-token input, 54,543 at two thousand, 50,302 at four thousand. Decode is nine nodes at EP72, 22,282 tokens per second at batch 256, two-thousand-token input. With simulated MTP and slow attention, decode at four-thousand-token input drops to 17,373.

Jenny: How close is that to DeepSeek's own profile?

Alex: SGLang prefill within 5.6 percent of DeepSeek's official profile. Decode within 6.6 percent under simulated MTP. Latency — TTFT two to five seconds, ITL around 100 milliseconds.

Jenny: TTFT two to five seconds is throughput-optimized, not latency-optimized.

Alex: That's exactly how LMSYS frames it. Throughput-optimized. If you want low TTFT for chat, this is not the configuration you'd ship. But for a max-throughput batch-inference workload, the numbers are real and reproducible.

Jenny: Okay so what are the honest limitations LMSYS calls out?

Alex: Several. Sequence length is constrained by the 96-GPU envelope — longer sequences need a bigger cluster. MTP integration with DP attention is incomplete — SGLang supports MTP but lacks full integration with DP attention, which reduces efficiency in mixed parallelism configs. EPLB was tuned on in-distribution data, may not reflect real-world variability. TP flexibility is limited — only pure TP or pure DP, the memory-optimal mid-range with TP between two and six is unavailable. And it's Hopper-only at the time of writing — Blackwell support is pending.

Jenny: Each of those is a real production gotcha.

Alex: Each of those is a real gotcha. Now — why prefill-decode disaggregation for MoE is harder than for dense, because this comes up.

Jenny: Yeah, because for a dense model, disaggregation is just, run prefill on box A, ship the KV cache to box B for decode.

Alex: Right. For a dense model, prefill and decode stress the same weights. The disaggregation choice is purely about batch shape — wide batches for prefill, many concurrent users for decode. Same weights, different schedule. For MoE, prefill and decode stress different experts. Prefill is a wide compute sweep over many tokens with broad expert coverage. Decode is per-token expert selection driven by what the user is generating.

Jenny: So the same model, but different parts of it are hot in each phase.

Alex: Different parts of it are hot in each phase, and the optimal placement differs. DeepSeek's two-stage topology — EP32 at prefill with thirty-two redundant experts, EP320 at decode — is the production answer to that asymmetry. The EP rank itself is part of the disaggregation, not just the GPU pool. And that's a deeper change than dense disaggregation.

Jenny: Okay. Memory pressure and cold start.

Alex: Even at FP8, seven hundred gigabytes is too large for any single 8-by-H100 node. Multi-node aggregation is the starting condition. Cold start is loading 700 GB of MoE weights and pre-compiling expert routing tables. The cited sources don't give specific cold-start numbers, but the MoE-specific component is real. Capturing CUDA graphs for variable-batch MoE is harder than for dense, because the batch shape inside each expert depends on the gate's runtime decisions.

Jenny: Right. CUDA graphs assume deterministic shapes. MoE breaks that assumption.

Alex: MoE breaks that assumption every layer. So the typical answer is shape padding — round the dynamic batch up to one of a few captured shapes — which costs you some compute but lets you reuse the graph. It's the same tradeoff as branch prediction. Pay a little extra for predictability, save a lot in scheduling overhead.

Jenny: Okay. Other realities?

Alex: Batch composition skew. With small batches, many experts sit idle. EPLB handles popularity skew at the placement level, but it cannot manufacture batch density that the traffic doesn't have. If your batch only activates four experts out of 256, your other 252 experts are dead weight for that batch, and EPLB can't conjure traffic that doesn't exist.

Jenny: That's a workload-mix problem, not a system problem.

Alex: It's a workload-mix problem. Heterogeneous traffic — prefill-heavy workloads like RAG or long context want different EP topologies than decode-heavy workloads like chat. The DeepSeek topology, 32-GPU prefill / 320-GPU decode, is one fixed answer. Production fleets need to flex across workload mixes, and the public sources don't describe a complete answer to that.

Jenny: Fault tolerance for the all-to-all.

Alex: Fault tolerance for the all-to-all is the one that scares me. When one expert worker dies, the entire synchronous all-to-all collective hangs until it's replaced. This is a direct consequence of the synchronous-blocking property from segment two. The public sources do not yet describe a complete answer.

Jenny: Hangs as in, the whole collective deadlocks until you swap the worker.

Alex: Until you swap the worker, or restart the collective. Which means your blast radius for a single GPU failure is the whole EP group. At EP320, that's three hundred and twenty GPUs.

Jenny: Three hundred and twenty GPUs going idle on one failure. That is a really uncomfortable production property.

Alex: It's uncomfortable. And if you're an EM looking at this, it's the kind of property that drives investment — what does fast detection look like, what does fast replacement look like, can you preemptively replicate state to a hot spare. The public literature doesn't have a complete answer yet.

Jenny: Quantization sensitivity is the last one.

Alex: Quantization sensitivity. Experts are individually smaller than dense weights, so per-expert quantization error compounds differently. TRT-LLM's blog 1 keeps attention at BF16 but quantizes dense FFN and MoE layers to FP4 — that mixed-precision recipe is the cited production choice for B200. It's not a general rule. It's a workload-and-hardware-specific calibration.

Jenny: So you can't just say, FP4 the whole thing and call it a day.

Alex: You absolutely cannot. You have to calibrate per-layer, validate quality on representative tasks, and ship the recipe that holds up. None of these realities — batch composition skew, heterogeneous traffic, fault tolerance, quantization sensitivity — show up in the four research papers. They show up the moment a deployment is more than one experiment.

Jenny: Which is why segment six matters as much as segment three.

Jenny: Okay one last thing — for the listener thinking about this in the AWS context, what's the takeaway?

Alex: I think the takeaway is, this is an auto-scaling problem in a regime you haven't encountered before. Your routing key is recomputed every layer. Your hot shards regenerate every batch. Your fault domain for a single GPU is the whole EP group. Your validation matrix is the cross product of model, hardware, parallelism, precision, topology. The compute primitive is familiar — it's a sparse matmul behind a dispatch — but the systems primitive is new.
