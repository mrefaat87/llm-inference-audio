Alex: So Jenny, last episode we talked about autoscaling — min replicas, max replicas, queue depth as the leading indicator. But there's a problem we kind of waved at, and it's the thing that keeps inference platform leads up at night.

Jenny: Let me guess. You set max replicas to a hundred, traffic spikes, and AWS goes "yeah, we don't actually have a hundred H100s for you right now."

Alex: Exactly that. The dirty secret of running inference at scale is that no single cloud provider has enough flagship GPUs to host your full footprint at on-demand prices. Not AWS, not GCP, not Azure. During peak demand windows, H100 SXM and GB200 capacity is just gone.

Jenny: Wait, hang on. AWS is enormous. They have entire regions. How is that even possible?

Alex: Two things. First, the demand for these chips is genuinely insane right now — every AI company is fighting for the same SKUs. Second, hyperscalers allocate their flagship GPU capacity to their biggest reserved-capacity customers first. If you're paying on-demand, you're at the back of the line.

Jenny: Okay, so what do people actually do? Because "sorry, no GPUs today" is not an acceptable answer to your CEO.

Alex: This is where multi-cloud bin packing comes in. You build a control plane that knows about replicas across AWS, GCP, a neocloud like CoreWeave or Lambda, and maybe your own colo. When traffic comes in, it routes to wherever you have capacity.

Jenny: Hold on, neocloud — that's a term I keep hearing. What makes them different from AWS?

Alex: Think of neoclouds as GPU specialists. CoreWeave, Lambda, Nebius, Crusoe — they don't try to be a full cloud. They don't have a hundred and fifty services. They have GPUs, networking between GPUs, and storage for model weights. That's the product.

Jenny: So they're like... a butcher shop next to a Whole Foods. Narrower selection, but the meat counter is way better.

Alex: That's actually a great analogy. And because they're specialists, they often have flagship GPUs available when AWS literally cannot give you any. They're also typically thirty to fifty percent cheaper per GPU-hour.

Jenny: Okay, so why isn't everyone just using neoclouds? What's the catch?

Alex: Smaller region footprint. CoreWeave has a handful of regions; AWS has thirty-plus. The non-GPU tooling is less mature — networking, IAM, secrets management, all of that is rougher than what you're used to. And contract terms vary wildly. Some require multi-year commits, some are flexible.

Jenny: Got it. So the picture is: you use AWS for the regions and the IAM story, you use CoreWeave when AWS runs out, and you mix the two.

Alex: Right. And there are even more options in the mix. Spot capacity — AWS Spot, GCP Preemptible — gives you forty to seventy percent discounts but can be reclaimed with minutes' notice. Resellers like SF Compute aggregate capacity across providers. And if you're really at scale, on-prem in a colo gives you the best dollar-per-GPU once you've absorbed the capex.

Jenny: But spot for inference seems terrifying. You're mid-request, mid-stream, and AWS yanks the GPU?

Alex: Yeah, spot is really only safe for batch jobs, training runs, or stateless inference where you can fail-fast and retry on another replica within seconds. For latency-sensitive serving, spot is usually a bad fit.

Jenny: Okay, let me push on the multi-cloud thing. You said control plane. That sounds expensive in engineering terms. What does that actually cost you?

Alex: A lot. Identity has to span providers — your service in AWS needs to authenticate to a GPU running on CoreWeave. Networking has to span providers, which usually means VPN or private interconnect. Secrets, monitoring, deployment pipelines, observability — all of it has to work uniformly across clouds.

Jenny: So it's not "flip a switch and you're multi-cloud."

Alex: Not even close. Most teams start single-cloud, get to a few hundred GPUs, hit the capacity wall, and only then add a second provider. You don't do this for fun. You do it because the GPU constraint forces the issue.

Jenny: Makes sense. But once you've paid that cost, you get more than just capacity, right? You get redundancy.

Alex: Exactly. A regional outage at one provider doesn't kill your service. And there's a third benefit — geographic latency.

Jenny: Oh, this is the speed-of-light thing.

Alex: Yeah. Network latency at long distances is basically physics-limited. The rule of thumb I use: about five milliseconds of round-trip latency per timezone of distance. NY to LA is around seventy milliseconds round trip. NY to London is about seventy-five. SF to Tokyo is one-ten.

Jenny: Wait, that's huge. If my TTFT budget for a streaming LLM is two hundred milliseconds, I just burned a third of it on the network before the GPU even sees the request.

Alex: Right. Which is why for a global product, multi-region isn't optional. It's table stakes. You serve EU users from EU regions, US users from US regions — and "EU region" means whichever provider happens to have GPUs there. Sometimes that's AWS Frankfurt, sometimes it's CoreWeave Amsterdam.

Jenny: Okay so now we're multi-cloud, multi-region. Which means we have a lot more replicas, in a lot more places, than we ever had before. That feels like it brings us to the failure question.

Alex: This is the part most people get wrong. They think GPUs are reliable like CPUs. They're not. The Llama 3 paper has the number you should actually plan for — they ran sixteen thousand GPUs for fifty-four days during pretraining and had four hundred nineteen hardware interruptions.

Jenny: Let me do the math on that... that's roughly one failure per fifty thousand GPU-hours.

Alex: Exactly. So if you're running a steady-state thousand-GPU inference fleet, that's a hardware incident every fifty hours. Three to four a week.

Jenny: Three to four hardware failures a week. That's not "occasional weird thing," that's "this is happening to you constantly."

Alex: It's the steady state. Which means active-passive failover — the classic enterprise pattern where you have a hot standby waiting — is too slow. By the time you've detected and failed over, you've already missed your SLO. You need active-active across at least two zones, ideally two regions.

Jenny: This is exactly the cattle-not-pets thing. A failing replica gets drained, replaced, and the in-flight request retries somewhere else.

Alex: Within seconds. The whole system is designed around the assumption that any individual GPU will die, and the platform handles it transparently. If a single replica failure causes a customer-visible incident, your architecture is wrong.

Jenny: And presumably this is also why multi-cloud isn't just about capacity — it's also about provider-wide outages.

Alex: Yes. Provider-wide outages happen. AWS us-east-1 has had multi-hour outages that took down huge chunks of the internet. If your entire inference fleet is in one provider's one region, you are one bad day away from being a postmortem.

Jenny: Okay, so the takeaway: at scale, multi-cloud and multi-region aren't optimizations. They're the baseline architecture, driven by capacity constraints, latency physics, and the actual hardware failure rate.

Alex: That's the whole story. Single-cloud single-region is fine until it isn't, and the moment it isn't, you're scrambling to build a control plane during an incident.

Alex: Next we get into the stuff that lives on top of this multi-cloud, multi-region foundation — security and compliance. SOC 2, HIPAA, data residency. Jenny: Oh, so the bin-packing problem gets harder. It's not just "which provider has GPUs," it's "which provider has GPUs in a region this customer's contract allows."

Alex: Exactly. And then there's tenant isolation inside the inference path itself — per-tenant cache scoping, adapter selection, sandboxed tool execution. The multi-tenancy hazards specific to LLM serving that don't exist in normal web services.
