Alex: So Jenny, I want to talk about something that sounds boring on the surface — number formats. FP32, FP16, INT8 — just numbers, right? But here's the thing: the choice of number format is probably the single most consequential infrastructure decision in inference serving. It determines your memory footprint, your throughput ceiling, and whether you can even fit a model on a given GPU.

Jenny: Okay, I'll bite. But help me understand why I should care. Like, a number is a number — why does the format matter so much?

Alex: Think about it this way. You know how in networking you have different compression levels for video streaming? You can send raw uncompressed 4K, or you can encode it with H.264 at different quality levels. The raw version is perfect fidelity but eats your bandwidth alive. The compressed version looks almost the same to the viewer but moves through the pipe way faster.

Jenny: Right, so the model weights are like the video frames, and the number format is the codec quality setting.

Alex: Exactly. Every single weight in a neural network — and we're talking billions of them — is stored as a number. FP32 uses 32 bits per weight. FP16 uses 16. FP8 uses 8. INT4 uses just 4. So right there, switching from FP32 to FP8 means your model is one quarter the size in memory. That's the difference between needing one GPU and needing four.

Jenny: Okay, but you can't just chop bits off a number and expect it to still be the same number. What are you actually losing?

Alex: Great question, and this is where it gets interesting. Let me unpack what's inside a floating-point number, because once you see the structure, everything about quantization clicks. Every floating-point number has three parts: a sign bit — positive or negative, that's easy. An exponent — this sets the order of magnitude, the scale. And a mantissa — this sets the precision within that scale.

Jenny: Can you make that concrete?

Alex: Sure. Think of scientific notation. Avogadro's number: 6.022 times 10 to the 23rd. The exponent — 23 — tells you you're in the hundreds of sextillions. That's the ballpark. The mantissa — 6.022 — tells you where exactly you are within that ballpark. If I take away mantissa digits and just say 6 times 10 to the 23rd, I'm still in the right neighborhood, just less precise. If I take away exponent range and can only go up to 10 to the 4th, I literally cannot represent that number at all.

Jenny: Oh, so exponent bits give you range and mantissa bits give you precision. And different formats make different tradeoffs between those two.

Alex: Bingo. FP32 is the luxury sedan — 8 exponent bits, 23 mantissa bits. Massive range, tons of precision. FP16 cuts to 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits. You lose range and precision, but you cut memory in half.

Jenny: So what's BF16? I keep hearing about it in training contexts.

Alex: BF16 is a really clever hack. Google Brain designed it and the "B" literally stands for "Brain." It keeps all 8 exponent bits — same dynamic range as FP32 — but slashes the mantissa down to just 7 bits. So you get the same range as the full 32-bit format in half the memory.

Jenny: Why would you want range over precision?

Alex: During training, your gradients can be wildly different magnitudes. Early layers might have tiny gradients like 0.00001, while later layers have activations in the hundreds. If your format can't cover that range, numbers overflow or underflow and training just blows up. BF16 says: I'd rather represent all those scales coarsely than represent some of them precisely and lose others entirely.

Jenny: That makes total sense. It's like — I'd rather have a monitoring system that can see all my services at low resolution than one that gives me microsecond precision on half my fleet and is completely blind to the other half.

Alex: Perfect analogy. And this is why you see BF16 dominating training while FP16 shows up more in inference. During inference, you don't have crazy gradient dynamics. Your values are more predictable, so you'd rather have the extra precision FP16 gives you.

Jenny: Okay, so we've got the floating-point family covered. But then there's INT8 and INT4 — integers. No exponent, no mantissa. What's the deal?

Alex: This is where things get really important. Integer formats are a fixed linear scale. INT8 gives you exactly the integers from negative 128 to positive 127. Every step is the same size. There's no exponent to shift your range around.

Jenny: That sounds... extremely limiting. How can you represent neural network values that might be 0.001 and also 500?

Alex: Short answer: you can't. Not well. And this is the root of one of the biggest problems in quantization. Here's how it plays out. For weights, it actually works okay. Model weights tend to be fairly uniformly distributed — most of them clustered in a reasonable range without crazy outliers. So you can map that range to INT8 or even INT4 with calibration and it works.

Jenny: But?

Alex: But activations — the intermediate values flowing between layers during inference — are a totally different story. Activations have outliers. Consistently. Across basically every transformer architecture anyone has studied. You'll have a tensor where 99% of the values are between negative 1 and positive 1, but then a handful of values are at 50 or 100 or 500.

Jenny: And if you're mapping to INT8's range of negative 128 to 127...

Alex: You have to set your scale to cover the outlier at 500. Now each step in your INT8 is about 4 units wide. Every activation that was between 0 and 4? They all collapse to the same quantized value. You've just destroyed all the precision for 99% of your activations because of 1% outliers.

Jenny: Oh wow. That's like... imagine you're setting up auto scaling and you have one customer who sends 100x the traffic of everyone else. If you size your instance pool for that one customer, your bin packing for everyone else is terrible. All that wasted capacity.

Alex: That's a really apt comparison. And this is exactly why techniques like SmoothQuant, AWQ, and GPTQ exist. They're all solving this outlier problem in different ways. SmoothQuant literally redistributes the difficulty — it takes the outlier magnitude from the activations and shifts it into the weights, which handle non-uniform distributions better. It's smoothing out the activation distribution before quantizing.

Jenny: Hang on. So SmoothQuant is basically reshaping the traffic pattern to make the bin packing work better, rather than changing the bin size.

Alex: Exactly right. You're not changing the format — you're still using INT8. You're changing the data to fit the format better.

Jenny: So then what about FP8? If INT8 has this outlier problem, why does FP8 work?

Alex: This is the key insight. FP8 keeps an exponent. Even with only 4 exponent bits — that's the E4M3 variant — the floating-point format can represent both 0.001 and 500 in the same tensor. The exponent automatically scales. No manual calibration, no clever redistribution tricks. The format itself handles the dynamic range.

Jenny: So FP8 on an H100 just... works? No SmoothQuant gymnastics needed?

Alex: Pretty much. H100 has native FP8 Tensor Cores, so you get roughly 2x the compute throughput of FP16, you use a quarter the memory of FP32, and quality stays near-FP16 levels. It's kind of the best of all worlds for that hardware generation. The format handles the outlier problem through its exponent bits, and the hardware accelerates FP8 math natively.

Jenny: So why would anyone bother with INT8 and SmoothQuant if FP8 just solves it?

Alex: Hardware availability. Not everyone is running H100s. A100s don't have FP8 Tensor Cores, but they do have INT8 acceleration. So if you're serving on A100s, SmoothQuant with INT8 is your path to quantized inference. Different hardware, different format strategy.

Jenny: That makes sense. You optimize for the hardware you actually have, not the hardware you wish you had.

Alex: And then at the extreme end, you have INT4. Four bits. Negative 8 to positive 7 — just 16 possible values. That's what GPTQ and AWQ use for weight quantization. You're getting 8x compression versus FP32. The model that needed 28 gigs now fits in 3.5.

Jenny: But that's weights only, right? You wouldn't dare do INT4 on activations.

Alex: Right. Weights are compressible because they're static and relatively uniform. Activations are dynamic, have outliers, and need that range. So in practice you see mixed-precision schemes — INT4 weights with FP16 activations, or FP8 everything on H100.

Jenny: There's one more thing I want to touch on. KV cache quantization. That's the memory that stores attention state for active sequences, right? What format makes sense there?

Alex: KV cache is interesting because K and V vectors are already-computed outputs. They're more regular than raw activations — they've already been through the attention computation. So INT8 or FP8 both work well. And the payoff is huge: you quantize your KV cache to INT8 and suddenly you can fit twice as many concurrent sequences in the same GPU memory. That's directly doubling your serving capacity.

Jenny: That's like doubling your instance count without buying any new hardware. Same HBM, twice the concurrent users.

Alex: Exactly. And this is why I said at the top that number format choice drives everything. It's not an academic detail — it determines your memory budget, your throughput, your concurrent capacity, and your quality floor. Every quantization method we'll talk about later is just a strategy for picking the right format for each part of the model.

Jenny: So to recap what I'm taking away: floating-point formats trade exponent bits for range and mantissa bits for precision. Integer formats give up range entirely for simplicity and compression. The activation outlier problem is why naive INT quantization fails. And FP8 basically solves that problem through its exponent, but only on hardware that supports it.

Alex: That's a clean summary. And looking ahead — this is the foundation for everything in quantization. When we get into GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, and FP8 inference in detail, every single design decision they make traces back to what we covered today. Why does GPTQ use calibration data? Because INT4 can't handle outliers without knowing the distribution. Why does SmoothQuant work at all? Because it reshapes activations to remove the outliers before INT8 quantization. Why is FP8 the default on H100? Because the format handles dynamic range natively.

Jenny: So the number format isn't just a setting you pick. It's the constraint that shapes every optimization downstream.

Alex: That's exactly right. The bits aren't just storage — they're the language your hardware speaks. And if you want your model to run fast, you'd better be fluent in that language.
