Alex: So Jenny, I want you to imagine you're running an auto scaling group, and every time a new request comes in, you have no idea whether it's going to take ten milliseconds or ten minutes to process. How do you plan capacity for that?

Jenny: You don't. I mean, you can't, right? You'd end up either massively over-provisioning or constantly getting blindsided by requests that take way longer than expected. That's basically the nightmare scenario for any capacity planner.

Alex: Exactly. And that's the reality of LLM inference right now. When a request comes into your serving system, you genuinely do not know how many tokens the model is going to generate. It could be twenty tokens — a quick one-liner. Or it could be two thousand tokens — a full essay. And you won't find out until the model emits that special end-of-sequence token that says "I'm done."

Jenny: Okay wait, so every request is basically an open-ended commitment? That's like accepting an EC2 instance reservation without knowing if the customer is going to use it for five minutes or five months.

Alex: That's a perfect analogy, and it gets worse. Because these requests are sitting in batches together. Remember continuous batching — the city bus model? Well imagine your bus has limited seats, and one passenger says they're going two stops, but actually they're riding to the end of the line. They're blocking that seat for twenty times longer than you expected. Meanwhile, short-ride passengers are stuck waiting at the bus stop because the bus looks full.

Jenny: So short requests get stuck behind long ones. That's head-of-line blocking, basically.

Alex: It is exactly head-of-line blocking. And this cascades into everything. Your SLO feasibility checks can't work — you can't reject a request proactively if you don't know how long it'll take to decode. Your preemption decisions get dumb — you might evict a request that's ninety-five percent done because you didn't know it was almost finished. And your KV cache, which grows linearly with every token generated, becomes impossible to budget for. You're flying blind.

Jenny: Alright, so the answer is obviously predict the output length. But how? The whole point of these models is that their output is, you know, unpredictable. It's generative.

Alex: Right, and there's a spectrum of approaches here, each with different accuracy and cost tradeoffs. The simplest one is what most people do today — just use the max tokens parameter from the request. The user says "I want at most 500 tokens," so you budget for 500.

Jenny: But that's wildly conservative. If most responses are like fifty tokens, you're over-allocating by ten x on every request.

Alex: Exactly. It's an upper bound, nothing more. But it costs you zero latency to compute, so it's the default fallback. The next step up is a classifier — just bucket requests into short, medium, and long. You train a lightweight model on your historical data, and it looks at the prompt and says "this looks like a short response."

Jenny: How accurate is that?

Alex: For three buckets, you're looking at seventy to eighty percent accuracy. And it only costs about a millisecond of inference time. It's not precise, but it's enough for coarse scheduling decisions — like deciding which lane a request goes into.

Jenny: Okay, so you can get fancier. What about actually predicting the exact token count?

Alex: You can train a regression model to do that. You'll get a mean absolute error of maybe thirty to a hundred tokens, depending on your workload. Same ballpark latency cost — about a millisecond. This is useful for SLO feasibility checks where you need a specific number to plug into your "can I serve this in time" calculation.

Jenny: What about asking the model itself? Like, "hey, how long is your answer going to be?"

Alex: People have tried that — LLM self-prediction. You basically prepend something to the prompt that says "estimate how long your response will be." It works okay, maybe sixty to seventy percent accurate within plus or minus thirty percent. But the cost is brutal — you're doing an extra prefill pass through the entire model just to get a length estimate.

Jenny: So you're burning GPU compute on predicting the length instead of actually generating the answer. That only makes sense for really high-value requests where getting the scheduling right matters more than the overhead.

Alex: Exactly. But here's where it gets really interesting. There's an approach from a Berkeley and Microsoft paper called QLM — the Andes paper from 2024 — that kind of flips the whole problem on its head. Instead of trying to nail the prediction for each individual request, they use the Central Limit Theorem.

Jenny: Wait, the Central Limit Theorem? From like, intro stats?

Alex: The very same. Here's the insight. Your per-request prediction can be mediocre — it can be noisy, it can be off by a lot for individual requests. But if you have a batch of, say, thirty requests, the total number of tokens across that batch converges to the true total. The variance shrinks as one over square root of N. So your batch-level capacity planning can be very accurate even when individual predictions are garbage.

Jenny: Oh, that's clever. So you don't need to solve the hard problem of "exactly how long will this one request be." You just need the aggregate to be right. It's like — okay, in auto scaling, I don't need to predict exactly how much CPU each individual request will use. I just need the fleet-level utilization forecast to be accurate, and the law of large numbers does the rest.

Alex: That's exactly the same principle. And the coverage intervals they get are around ninety-five percent — meaning ninety-five percent of the time, the actual batch total falls within their predicted range. That's good enough for capacity planning.

Jenny: Okay, but what about scheduling individual requests? If I want shortest-job-first to reduce latency for quick requests, I do need per-request estimates.

Alex: You do, but here's a trick — you don't actually need the absolute length. You just need the relative ordering. Is request A shorter than request B? That's a much easier problem to learn. It's called learning-to-rank, and it's sufficient for shortest-job-first scheduling.

Jenny: Oh, like you don't need to know the exact ETA of every car on the highway. You just need to know which lane is moving faster.

Alex: Right. And then you add aging to prevent starvation. Because pure shortest-job-first has the classic problem — long requests can starve if short ones keep cutting in line. So you compute an effective priority that combines the shortness score with how long the request has been waiting. The longer it waits, the more its priority increases.

Jenny: That's literally the Linux CFS scheduler. Or weighted round-robin with priority aging. We do this exact thing in request routing.

Alex: It's the same pattern, just applied to token generation scheduling. And it works surprisingly well.

Jenny: Alright, so let's talk about failure modes. What happens when your predictions are wrong? Because they will be wrong.

Alex: There are two directions to be wrong, and they're very asymmetric. Over-prediction — you predicted long, but the request finishes early. That's the safe failure. You allocated more KV cache than you needed, you maybe held a batch slot open longer than necessary. But the request finishes, the resources free up. Minor inefficiency.

Jenny: And under-prediction is the dangerous one.

Alex: Very dangerous. You predicted short, so you put the request in the express lane, allocated a small KV cache budget. But then it keeps generating and generating. Now you have to either preempt it — evict it and restart it later, which destroys latency — or it blocks your express lane and violates SLOs for everything behind it. It's like telling an auto scaling group that a workload is small, so it gets placed on a small instance, and then it OOMs.

Jenny: So you want to be biased toward over-prediction for safety.

Alex: Exactly. The practical mitigation is to use the upper bound of your confidence interval for admission control, not the point estimate. If your model says "I think this request is two hundred tokens, but the ninety-fifth percentile is five hundred," you use five hundred for your admission check. You eat some over-provisioning, but you avoid the catastrophic under-prediction failures.

Jenny: That makes total sense. Same reason we use P99 latency for capacity planning instead of the average — the tail is where things break.

Alex: And here's the key insight that I think surprised me most about this whole area. Prediction accuracy matters way less than you'd expect. Even a noisy predictor — one that just correctly identifies the top twenty-five percent shortest and the bottom twenty-five percent longest requests — that's enough to get a two to three x reduction in average latency for short requests. You don't need to be right about everyone. You just need to separate the fast from the slow with reasonable accuracy.

Jenny: So the bar isn't "predict token count within ten." The bar is "don't put the ten-minute requests in the same lane as the ten-second requests."

Alex: That's it. Coarse separation is the eighty-twenty solution. And that's achievable with even simple classifiers.

Jenny: Okay, so looking ahead — we've been talking about predicting how long requests take so we can schedule them better. But scheduling is just one piece of the puzzle, right? If you can predict output length, that also feeds into how you route requests across different nodes, and it connects directly to something we haven't talked about yet — disaggregated inference, where you literally run different phases of generation on different hardware.

Alex: That's exactly where this leads. Once you can predict output length, you can make much smarter decisions about which requests go to your prefill nodes versus your decode nodes. You can pre-allocate KV cache on the decode side before the prefill even finishes. It turns your blind system into one that can actually plan ahead — like going from reactive auto scaling to predictive scaling.

Jenny: From CloudWatch alarms to forecast-based scaling. I like that. That's the next episode then — disaggregated inference, splitting the brain of the model across different hardware.

Alex: And you're going to love it, because it maps almost perfectly to read-write splitting in databases.
