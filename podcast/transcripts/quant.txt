Alex: So you know how when you're sizing EC2 instances, the first question is always "will this thing even fit in memory"? That's the whole story of quantization in one sentence.

Jenny: Okay, lay it on me.

Alex: A 70B parameter model in FP16 — sixteen-bit floats — is 140 gigabytes of weights. That doesn't fit on a single H100, which has 80 gigs of HBM. Same model in INT4? About 35 gigs. Suddenly it fits on one GPU with room to spare for KV cache and activations.

Jenny: So this isn't a "make it faster" optimization, this is a "can I even serve this thing on one box" optimization.

Alex: Exactly. It's the difference between needing a multi-node tensor-parallel deployment with all the NVLink complexity, versus a single-instance serving setup. That's a massive operational simplification before we even talk about latency.

Jenny: Okay so we're chopping precision. But there are like ten acronyms — AWQ, GPTQ, FP8, INT8, GGUF. Why so many?

Alex: They split along two axes. The first and biggest one: do you only quantize the stored weights, or do you also quantize the activations flowing between layers?

Jenny: What's the difference operationally?

Alex: If it's weight-only — that's AWQ, GPTQ, GGUF — the weights sit in HBM in INT4. But the moment you actually do the matrix multiply, the GEMM, you dequantize back up to FP16 and the math runs at FP16.

Jenny: Wait. So I save HBM and HBM bandwidth, because I'm pulling four times less data per decode step. But the compute speed itself doesn't change?

Alex: Right. Zero compute speedup. Now, that's actually fine for decode, because decode is memory-bandwidth-bound — you're streaming weights through the chip one token at a time, the GPU's compute is idle waiting on memory. So bandwidth savings translate directly to latency wins.

Jenny: Ah — so for decode, weight-only is basically free wins. What's the other approach?

Alex: Weight plus activation quantization. FP8, SmoothQuant INT8. Both the weights and the intermediate values flowing between layers stay low-precision. The GEMM itself runs at FP8 or INT8 end-to-end. On H100's Tensor Cores, an FP8 GEMM is about 2x the throughput of FP16. So now you save memory AND you get real compute speedup.

Jenny: Okay but if that's strictly better, why does anyone use weight-only?

Alex: Because activations are way harder to quantize than weights. Activation distributions have these vicious outliers — most values cluster near zero, but a few channels spike to massive magnitudes. If you naively map that range to INT8, the outliers force you to stretch the scale so wide that everything else collapses to zero.

Jenny: Oh — so the question isn't "what's the average magnitude," it's "what's the worst-case magnitude in this tensor"? That's a hot-shard problem. One noisy neighbor wrecks the whole representation.

Alex: That's exactly the right framing. And it leads to the second axis: granularity. How many values share one scale factor?

Jenny: Scale factor being…

Alex: When you quantize, you store the integer values plus one floating-point scale that maps them back to real numbers. Per-tensor means one scale for the entire weight matrix — millions of values. Per-channel means one scale per row or column. Per-block means one scale per group of, say, 32 elements.

Jenny: And the per-block one is basically: shrink the blast radius of an outlier.

Alex: Yes. With per-tensor, one bad value in row 5000 stretches the scale and crushes precision for every other value. With per-block of 32, that outlier only damages its own 32 neighbors. Industry has marched steadily that direction — per-tensor, per-channel, per-block 128, then 32, now 16 in NVFP4.

Jenny: Why not block size of 1? Just give every value its own scale?

Alex: Then you're back to FP16 — you've just reinvented floating point. The scale factors themselves cost storage. NVFP4 is interesting because Nvidia did the math and landed on 16-element blocks with FP8 scales, plus a global FP32 scale per tensor. Two-level scaling.

Jenny: Like a hierarchical cache. Cheap fine-grained scale absorbs local variation, coarse global scale handles the dynamic range.

Alex: Beautiful analogy, yeah. And on Blackwell that's native hardware — the Tensor Cores understand microscaling formats directly.

Jenny: Okay back to FP8 versus INT8. Why does FP8 handle outliers when INT8 doesn't?

Alex: FP8 keeps the floating-point structure — sign, exponent, mantissa. The exponent bits give you logarithmic dynamic range. So you can represent 0.001 and 500 in the same tensor without precision collapse. INT8 is linear — every value is just an integer times a single scale. Outliers wreck it.

Jenny: That's why FP8 works without calibration data, but INT8 needs SmoothQuant.

Alex: Right. SmoothQuant is a clever hack for the A100 era. A100 doesn't have FP8 hardware, it has INT8 Tensor Cores. So if you want compute speedup on A100, you have to make INT8 work. The trick: multiply the activations by a per-channel scale s to shrink the outlier channels, and divide the corresponding weight column by s. Mathematically identical product, but now both distributions are tame enough for INT8.

Jenny: That's just migrating the difficulty from activations to weights. Why is that a win?

Alex: Because weight distributions are way more regular than activation distributions. Activations depend on input data — wild and bursty. Weights are learned and smooth. So you're moving the hard problem onto the surface that handles it better.

Jenny: That's a beautiful piece of judo. Okay — AWQ versus GPTQ. Both INT4, both weight-only.

Alex: GPTQ does a layer-by-layer least-squares optimization. For each layer, it asks: what quantized weights minimize the change to this layer's output on a calibration dataset? It uses the Hessian — second-order curvature info — to identify which weights are most sensitive and round those most carefully.

Jenny: Sensitivity-aware bin packing.

Alex: Pretty much. AWQ takes a different angle. It says: not all weight channels are equally important. The channels that multiply against large activation magnitudes carry more signal. Identify those salient channels via a calibration pass, scale them up before quantizing so they use more of the INT4 range, and apply the inverse at inference — which gets folded into an adjacent op for free.

Jenny: So GPTQ asks "which weights are mathematically sensitive," AWQ asks "which weights see the biggest activations in practice." And AWQ generally wins on quality?

Alex: At INT4, yes — especially on reasoning tasks. And AWQ needs less calibration data — 32 to 128 samples versus GPTQ's 128 to 512. Calibration for a 70B model is 10 to 30 minutes on AWQ, 1 to 4 hours on GPTQ.

Jenny: Hold on. Calibration. That's not training, right?

Alex: No — the model isn't learning. You're just running representative data through it to observe how each weight or channel actually behaves, then choosing your quantization parameters based on those observations.

Jenny: And here's the trap, right? If I calibrate on English Wikipedia and serve Korean traffic, I'm going to get bad quality on Korean.

Alex: Exactly the failure mode. Two to five perplexity points worse on low-resource and morphologically complex languages. The fix is cheap — mix 10 to 20 percent target-distribution samples into calibration. But you have to know to do it.

Jenny: Calibration data is a production config that needs to match your traffic. Got it. What about QAT — quantization-aware training?

Alex: PTQ — post-training — is what we've been discussing. Take an FP16 checkpoint, calibrate, ship. Cheap. QAT bakes the quantization noise into training itself. Forward pass simulates low-precision arithmetic, gradients still flow at FP32, the model learns weights that are robust to the quantization grid. Way more expensive but basically zero quality gap.

Jenny: And frontier models are starting to ship natively quantized?

Alex: This is the big shift. OpenAI's GPT-OSS shipped in MXFP4 — there is no FP16 reference checkpoint. DeepSeek V3 was trained end-to-end in FP8 mixed precision. Kimi K2 Thinking is INT4 with QAT. Your operational question changes from "how aggressively can I quantize this?" to "what's the lowest-precision format the model already supports?"

Jenny: That's a serving-side question now, not an ML-research question. Match the engine and hardware to what the model already is.

Alex: One more thing worth flagging — the sensitivity ladder. Not every part of a model tolerates quantization equally. FFN weights are easy — INT4 is fine. Activations are medium — FP8 is fine, INT8 needs SmoothQuant. KV cache is sensitive in long contexts because errors compound across decode steps. And softmax inside attention is universally exempted — you keep that in BF16 or FP16 because tiny score differences cascade into wrong token choices.

Jenny: So a "FP8 model" in production is really FP8 weights, FP8 activations, FP8 KV cache, BF16 attention math. It's a precision mosaic.

Alex: Precision mosaic, exactly. TensorRT Model Optimizer automates the per-tensor decision.

Because everything we just talked about — FP8, INT4, microscaling — is meaningless without a kernel that can actually execute that math at hardware speed. Marlin for INT4, the FP8 paths in CUTLASS, the fused attention kernels. The format is the contract; the kernel is the implementation.

Jenny: So quantization picks the format, kernels make it fast, and if the kernel doesn't exist for your format you get nothing.

Alex: That's the whole game. You can have the most beautifully quantized weights in the world — if there's no fast GEMM kernel for that format on your hardware, you're just moving smaller numbers slowly.
