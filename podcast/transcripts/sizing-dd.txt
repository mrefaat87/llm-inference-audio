Alex: So here's the thing. You already know vLLM. You know PagedAttention, you know continuous batching, you know prefix caching gives you a free win when there's a shared system prompt. You've probably tuned `gpu_memory_utilization` on a real deployment.

Jenny: Right.

Alex: But if I asked you — for a brand new model, on brand new hardware, with a brand new SLO contract — how many GPUs do I need, what topology, what batch size, what attention variant — could you answer that on a napkin before you spun anything up?

Jenny: Honestly? No. I'd benchmark. I'd just throw it on a box and start sweeping.

Alex: Exactly. And that is the gap this whole episode is about. There's a framework — it's mostly in the Google DeepMind Scaling Book, Parts 7 and 8 — that lets you do the napkin math first. You can predict, before you touch a GPU, which regime you'll be in, what your bottleneck will be, and roughly what the achievable throughput is. And it generalizes. It works for DeepSeek-V3 on H800s, it works for Llama on H100s, it works for Character.AI's stack at twenty thousand QPS.

Jenny: Okay so this is "auto scaling instincts, for inference."

Alex: That's literally the right framing, and I want to start there, because if you don't have the mental model right, none of the numbers stick. Here's the move. Forget tokens for a second. Think of an inference deployment as an Auto Scaling Group. What are your fixed costs and what are your variable costs?

Jenny: Fixed cost is what's running when zero requests are in flight. Variable cost is what scales with concurrency.

Alex: Map that to LLM serving. What's the fixed cost?

Jenny: The model weights sitting in HBM. They're paid for whether you have one request or a thousand.

Alex: Right. The weights are your warm instance pool. They're sunk. The variable cost is the KV cache, because that grows with concurrent sessions times context length. So your job — and this is going to sound flippant but it's literally true — your job as the person sizing the deployment is exactly the same job as the person sizing an ASG. Size the fleet to meet SLO at the lowest dollar per million tokens.

Jenny: Okay but in an ASG I have like, target tracking on CPU utilization. What's the equivalent here?

Alex: HBM bandwidth utilization. Because — and we're going to spend the next ten minutes on this — decode is almost always memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. So your "CPU at 70%" target is actually "HBM bandwidth at 80%." If you're not pushing the memory bus, you're leaving throughput on the floor.

Jenny: Wait. That's a real inversion. In every distributed system I've ever sized, compute is the thing I'm watching. You're telling me on these chips the FLOPs are basically idle?

Alex: On the decode side, often yes. The H100 has roughly a petaflop of fp8 FLOPs and about 3.35 terabytes per second of HBM bandwidth. Divide those — you get this ratio of about 295 FLOPs per byte in bf16, or 590 in fp8. That's the roofline. That ratio is the number that decides everything.

Jenny: Walk me through that ratio. What is it telling me?

Alex: It's telling you the break-even point. If your kernel does fewer than 295 FLOPs per byte it loads from HBM, you finish the FLOPs before the memory arrives. So your time is set by memory bandwidth. If you do more than 295, you finish the memory load first and your time is set by FLOPs. There's a name for that ratio for a workload — arithmetic intensity. FLOPs per byte moved.

Jenny: Ops per I-O. That's exactly the metric I'd use for a storage tier.

Alex: It's the same model. It's literally the same plot. You can draw the roofline diagram for HBM the way you'd draw it for a buffer pool in a database. Below the ridge, you're bandwidth-limited and throughput grows linearly with intensity. Above the ridge, you're compute-limited and it's flat. Same shape.

Jenny: Okay so 295 FLOPs per byte on H100 bf16. What's a real workload look like? A decoder doing one token at a time — what's its arithmetic intensity?

Alex: For decode attention, basically one. You load the KV cache and you do one dot product per element. So you're at about one FLOP per byte against a roofline of 295. You're using a third of a percent of your compute. The GPU is mostly waiting on HBM.

Jenny: That's brutal.

Alex: That's why batching exists. Every request you add to the batch shares the weight load — you stream the weights from HBM once and apply them to B requests' worth of activations. So your arithmetic intensity is, roughly, the batch size. Batch one, AI of one. Batch a hundred, AI of a hundred. Batch 295... 

Jenny: You hit the ridge.

Alex: You hit the ridge. And that's the critical batch size — B-crit. The Scaling Book writes it as peak FLOPs over peak HBM bandwidth, times a correction for the ratio of bits per parameter to bits per activation. On H100 bf16 weights with bf16 activations, B-crit is about 280 tokens. Below 280 you're memory-bound, above 280 you're compute-bound.

Jenny: And below 280, what happens to latency as I add requests?

Alex: This is the magical part. Latency is flat. Because you were going to pay the weight load anyway, whether you had one request or two hundred. So adding requests in the memory-bound regime is free throughput.

Jenny: Free.

Alex: Free. Until you cross the ridge. Then every new request costs you proportional latency. So the rule is: if you're throughput-optimizing, you want to batch as close to B-crit as you can. If you're latency-optimizing, you want to batch below it. Reiner Pope phrased it on the Dwarkesh interview as — below B-crit, latency is flat as you add requests; above, latency grows linearly with batch. If you remember one curve from this whole episode, make it that one.

Jenny: Hold on. You said int8 weights drop B-crit to 140. Why is that?

Alex: Because the FLOP roofline depends on the precision of your matmuls. Int8 or fp8 doubles your FLOPs throughput on H100 — Tensor Cores are twice as fast in 8-bit as in 16-bit — but HBM bandwidth is the same. So the ratio of FLOPs per byte doubles to 590. But — and this is the subtlety — B-crit also has that correction term, bits per param over bits per activation. If your weights are int8 and your activations are still bf16, the parameter byte is half the cost, so you reach saturation at half the batch. B-crit drops to 140.

Jenny: So smaller B-crit means I get to compute-bound faster, with less batching.

Alex: Right. And if you also push activations to int8, the FLOP roofline doubles but so does the activation savings, and B-crit goes back to 280. The two effects cancel.

Jenny: Okay that's neat. And H200?

Alex: H200 is interesting. Same peak FLOPs as H100 — 9.9 times ten to the fourteen — but HBM bandwidth went from 3.35 to 4.8 terabytes per second. So the FLOPs-per-byte ratio actually goes down, to about 205 in bf16. You saturate at a smaller batch on H200 than on H100.

Jenny: Which means for latency-sensitive serving, H200 is actually the easier chip to keep in the latency-friendly regime.

Alex: That's the read. You get more bandwidth, you reach saturation sooner, you can run smaller batches without giving up throughput. That's the entire upgrade story.

Jenny: Okay. Now you keep saying decode is memory-bound and prefill is something else. Walk me through prefill.

Alex: Prefill is the same model, opposite regime. In prefill you process the whole prompt in parallel — the prompt comes in, you run it through every layer, you produce the first KV state, and you emit the first token. So the per-step work isn't "one token, one matmul of width B" — it's "T tokens, one matmul of width T." Suddenly your arithmetic intensity is on the order of T over 2. If T is more than about 480 tokens, prefill is compute-bound.

Jenny: So prefill is the FLOPs-eating part and decode is the bandwidth-eating part.

Alex: Yes. Same weights, same model, totally different rooflines. And here's the punchline. If you mix them on the same server, every time a new prompt lands you have to interrupt the decode loop to do a compute-bound prefill burst, and that craters your time-between-tokens for every request currently decoding. Your p99 TBT goes to hell.

Jenny: That's what chunked prefill is trying to fix in vLLM, right? You slice the prefill across multiple steps.

Alex: Right. Chunked prefill is the band-aid that lets you keep both phases on one server. But the structural answer is to disaggregate. Run prefill on a pool optimized for compute — small batches, high TP, push FLOPs — and decode on a pool optimized for bandwidth — big batches, high concurrency, push HBM utilization. Ship the KV cache between them.

Jenny: That's literally separating the two regimes so each pool gets its own roofline.

Alex: That's the whole game. And the entire mechanical case for disaggregated serving — DeepSeek does it, LMSYS reproduces it, Character.AI does it — comes from that observation. Prefill and decode want opposite batch shapes.

Jenny: Okay. Roofline. B-crit. Two regimes. I think I have it. But weights are only one part of what's streaming through HBM, right? You keep mentioning KV.

Alex: That's the next big thing. Once your weights fit, KV cache is the new bottleneck. And the reason is that, on every single decode step, you stream the entire KV cache for every active sequence through HBM for the attention kernel. Every step. So your bandwidth-bound regime now has two consumers: weights, plus KV times batch.

Jenny: And KV scales with batch and sequence length.

Alex: Linearly in both. So as you raise the batch — which you want to do for throughput — you raise the per-step bytes by B times KV-per-token. At some point that dominates the weight load entirely, and adding more batch stops helping because step time grows just as fast as throughput.

Jenny: What's the formula? Give me the napkin version.

Alex: KV per token equals two — that's for K and V — times bytes per element, times number of KV heads, times head dim, times number of layers. The two is the K and V. The bytes-per-element is your dtype — two for bf16, one for int8 or fp8. The KV heads is where the attention variant matters.

Jenny: Right, GQA shares heads.

Alex: GQA, MQA, MLA — they're all answers to the question "how much can I share K and V across query heads without tanking quality." Let me give you the table that lives rent-free in everyone's head. Take a DeepSeek-V3-shaped model — 61 layers, 128 heads, head dim 128. At 32K context, MHA — vanilla multi-head — costs you 256 gigabytes of KV per single sequence. Per user.

Jenny: That's more than three H100s for one user.

Alex: For one user. That's why nobody actually runs MHA at 32K. GQA with 8 groups — which is what Llama 3 uses — drops it to 16 gigabytes per sequence. MQA, which is one shared K and one shared V across all heads, gets you down to 2 gigabytes. And MLA, DeepSeek's invention, is 2.18 gigabytes.

Jenny: MLA matches MQA on size but DeepSeek says it's higher quality. How?

Alex: This is the clever bit. MLA doesn't actually store separate K and V per head. It stores a single low-rank latent vector — one compressed representation — plus a small RoPE component for positional info. The total cached state is small. But when you read it back, you up-project that latent through a learned matrix to reconstruct all the multi-head K and V you need for that step. So you cache compressed, decompress on read.

Jenny: That's literally compressed columnar storage. You're paying decompression cost on every read in exchange for storage size.

Alex: That's a great analogy. And the question with any compression scheme is whether the decompression cost outruns the bandwidth savings. With MLA, it doesn't, because you were memory-bound anyway. You had FLOPs to burn. So you spend FLOPs on the up-projection and you save bandwidth on the cache stream. Net win.

Jenny: And the quality thing?

Alex: The DeepSeek-V2 paper makes the case. MLA preserves multi-head expressivity at inference because you really do produce a full set of heads on every read — they're just derived from a shared latent. MQA collapses to a single head, which is genuinely losing capacity. MLA keeps the heads, it just shares their substrate. The V2 paper shows MLA beating GQA at the same cache budget. That's the entire reason DeepSeek invented it.

Jenny: Okay, let me put this in numbers. If I'm running Llama 3 70B with GQA-8, what does the math look like?

Alex: Scaling Book Part 8 does this exact walkthrough. Llama 3 70B: hidden dim 8192, 80 layers, 64 query heads but 8 KV heads — that's the GQA-8 — head dim 128. KV per token in int8: 2 times 8 KV heads times 128 head dim times 80 layers. Comes out to 160 kilobytes per token.

Jenny: 160K per token. So a single 8K sequence is...

Alex: 1.3 gigabytes. Batch 32 at 8K context is 42 gigabytes of KV on top of 70 gigabytes of int8 weights. That's 112 gigabytes total. Doesn't fit on one chip. The book sizes it as a 4-by-2 TPU v5e replica — eight chips together.

Jenny: And what's the throughput?

Alex: 17 milliseconds per decode step, 1,882 tokens per second aggregate. 235 tokens per second per chip on a 4-by-2 v5e replica at batch 32, int8. And the choice of batch 32 was deliberate. B-crit on v5e with int8 weights and bf16 activations is 120. They chose 32 on purpose to stay deep in the memory-bound regime, so that adding sessions would raise throughput without raising per-step latency.

Jenny: They're sitting comfortably under the ridge so they have room.

Alex: They're sitting comfortably under the ridge. They could push to 120 if they wanted more throughput per chip, at the cost of latency starting to climb. That's the kind of decision the framework helps you make in advance, on paper, before you launch a benchmark.

Jenny: Let me ask the obvious question. PagedAttention. We both know vLLM allocates KV in blocks. What's the right block size and why?

Alex: vLLM defaults to 16 tokens per block. And the way to think about it is exactly virtual memory paging. You have a free list of physical blocks, each block stores 16 tokens worth of KV across all heads and layers, and per request you maintain a list of which physical blocks back this request's logical context. When the request grows, you allocate another block off the free list. When the request finishes, you return its blocks.

Jenny: So why 16 and not 1, or 256?

Alex: Two tensions. Smaller block size means more allocation overhead and more fragmentation. The Inside vLLM writeup walks through this — the per-block metadata starts to dominate at small sizes, and if you're trying to do prefix caching, smaller blocks mean alignment breaks more easily and you can't reuse partial blocks across prefixes.

Jenny: Like 4K vs 1MB pages.

Alex: Same tradeoff. Bigger block size means less fragmentation overhead but more wasted tail-of-sequence. If your sequence ends mid-block, the rest of that block is wasted memory. So 16 is the empirical sweet spot for the kinds of sequences and the kinds of prefix patterns vLLM was tuned for. You can override it but most people don't.

Jenny: Okay so we've talked about weights, we've talked about KV. We've talked about why decode and prefill want opposite things. What's the parallelism story? How do you actually shard this across a node, across a rack?

Alex: This is where most people make their worst decision. Because their auto scaling reflex is data parallelism — just replicate the whole thing. And data parallelism is useless for decode latency.

Jenny: Why?

Alex: Because it doesn't speed up a single step. Data parallelism gives you more independent replicas, so you can serve more concurrent requests, but each replica still has to stream the weights through HBM at the same rate. DP doesn't divide your bandwidth bill. It just gives you more bandwidth bills to pay.

Jenny: It's horizontal scaling without sharding.

Alex: Right. And what you need is sharding of the weights themselves so each GPU has less weight to stream per step. That's tensor parallelism. Megatron-style TP shards the hidden dimension of every matmul. So if you do TP-4 on a layer, each GPU does a quarter of that layer's work and you all-reduce at the end of the layer.

Jenny: And each GPU only streams a quarter of the weights.

Alex: Each GPU streams a quarter of the weights per step. So per-step decode latency gets divided by your TP degree. That's the only sharding pattern that helps decode latency. But TP has limits — the all-reduce overhead grows with the number of participants, and it's only viable inside an NVLink island.

Jenny: How big is an NVLink island?

Alex: On H100, eight GPUs per node connected by NVLink at hundreds of gigabytes per second of one-way bandwidth. So TP-8 is practical inside one node. TP across nodes is bottlenecked by inter-node bandwidth — Infiniband at 50 gigabytes per second, much slower — and the all-reduce starts to dominate. Don't go TP across nodes unless you really have to.

Jenny: What if the model doesn't fit in one node?

Alex: Then you reach for pipeline parallelism. Stack the layers across nodes. Each node owns a contiguous block of layers. Tokens flow through. PP is great for throughput because it pipelines across micro-batches. But it adds latency — you have pipeline bubbles, especially at the start and end of a batch.

Jenny: So PP only when you have to.

Alex: PP only when the model plus its KV genuinely doesn't fit in one node. And then there's expert parallelism — EP — but that's specifically for mixture of experts. You shard the experts across GPUs so each GPU owns some subset of the experts. We'll get to that with DeepSeek.

Jenny: And sequence parallelism?

Alex: SP shards the sequence dimension. Useful for very long context prefill, basically useless for single-token decode. So for decode you have TP and that's it as a useful weight-sharding pattern. The Scaling Book derives a sweet spot for TP. Y-max equals F over B-crit times beta, where beta is the ratio of HBM bandwidth to interconnect bandwidth. On v5e it's about 8.

Jenny: So there's a maximum useful TP degree.

Alex: There's a maximum useful TP degree before the interconnect becomes the bottleneck instead of HBM. And the interesting move — which the book recommends explicitly for latency-bound serving — is to push TP past the FLOP-utilization optimum, intentionally wasting some FLOPs in exchange for lower per-step time.

Jenny: You're trading utilization for time-to-token. That's why a hand-tuned decode deployment looks "wasteful" by training standards.

Alex: That's exactly right. Training cares about FLOPs per dollar. Latency-bound serving cares about time per token. Different optimizers, different optima.

Jenny: Can you give me the closed form? You teased it earlier.

Alex: In the memory-bound regime, step time is approximately the bytes you stream per step divided by your bandwidth. The bytes per step are batch times KV-per-sequence — that's the KV streaming — plus weight params. Divide by total HBM bandwidth and that's your step time. Tokens per second is batch divided by step time, which gives you batch times bandwidth, over batch-times-KV plus weights.

Jenny: So at small batch, weights dominate the denominator and throughput scales linearly with batch.

Alex: Linearly. Free throughput. Then as batch grows, batch-times-KV starts to dominate, and throughput asymptotes. The crossover happens when KV bytes equal weight bytes per step. That's the diminishing-returns knee on every "throughput vs ITL" sweep graph you've ever seen.

Jenny: And past that knee, doubling the batch buys less than doubling the throughput.

Alex: Less than 2x per doubling. The classic asymptote. You can sketch this curve on paper before launching a single benchmark, which is enormously powerful. You can predict your own results.

Jenny: Okay. So I think I have the math. Roofline, B-crit, KV formula, parallelism. Let me hear how this plays out in real deployments. You keep mentioning DeepSeek and Character.AI and the LMSYS reproduction.

Alex: Three case studies, three very different corners of the same Pareto frontier. Let's start with DeepSeek-V3, because that's the one with the most going on. 671 billion parameters total, 37 billion activated per token — MoE. 256 experts plus 32 redundant. MLA for attention. FP8 mixed precision end-to-end. Trained on 2,048 H800 GPUs.

Jenny: H800, not H100. The bandwidth-limited export variant.

Alex: Right, half the NVLink bandwidth of H100, capped Infiniband. So they had to design the system around the network constraints from day one. Four architectural decisions and every single one was a hardware co-design move. First — MLA. We covered why. Matches MQA cache size with multi-head quality. Second — FP8 everywhere. They train in FP8, they serve in FP8. That doubles their FLOPs roofline from 295 to 590 on Hopper, halves memory consumption versus BF16, and cuts the expert all-to-all communication volume by 50% because the activations on the wire are 8-bit instead of 16-bit.

Jenny: That last one is huge for an MoE.

Alex: That last one is the entire story for an MoE at scale. MoE inference latency is dominated by all-to-all communication — when each token's chosen experts are scattered across different GPUs, you do a network shuffle to send each token to its experts and another shuffle to bring the results back. Twice per layer. Synchronous. So halving the bytes on the wire is the biggest perf lever you have.

Jenny: That's literally the same as compressing payloads before a shuffle in a distributed sort.

Alex: Same exact play. Third decision — MTP, multi-token prediction, at training time. They train extra heads to predict the next-plus-one, next-plus-two, etc. Then at inference, they repurpose those heads as a built-in speculative decoder. The acceptance rate they report is 80 to 90 percent on the second token, which gives 1.8x effective generation throughput.

Jenny: Speculative decoding gets a free ride because you were bandwidth-bound anyway.

Alex: Exactly the right intuition. Spec decode trades FLOPs for bandwidth wins. Your verifier model has to run a forward pass on the draft tokens, which costs FLOPs. But on a memory-bound decoder, those FLOPs were idle. So it's almost free FLOPs in exchange for fewer step-by-step weight loads. Pure win.

Jenny: And the fourth?

Alex: The fourth is networking — a multi-plane two-layer fat tree replacing the conventional three-layer. Isolates collective communication from request traffic, cuts cluster networking costs. The DeepSeek insights paper makes the point explicitly: they redesigned the network topology around the MoE all-to-all pattern.

Jenny: They co-designed the data center.

Alex: They co-designed the data center. That's the level of vertical integration we're talking about. And then in deployment, they disaggregate prefill from decode and use wildly different parallelism for each. Prefill runs TP4 plus sequence parallelism, EP32, on 32 GPUs across 4 nodes. Decode runs EP320 on 320 GPUs across 40 nodes.

Jenny: Wait. EP320. They shard experts across 320 GPUs?

Alex: Wide expert parallelism. They have 256 routed experts, so EP320 means most GPUs hold a single expert, plus some redundant copies of the popular ones. The all-to-all spans the entire decode pool. This is only sane because the H800 NVLink fabric — even at half the H100 bandwidth — can absorb the shuffle, and because their custom DeepEP kernels are tuned for exactly this topology.

Jenny: And the load balancing problem? Some experts get picked more than others.

Alex: This is huge. The MoE gate routes each token to its top-k experts based on learned routing scores, and the distribution is never uniform. Some experts are "hot." DeepSeek has two mechanisms. First, auxiliary-loss-free balancing — they learn a routing bias that pushes tokens toward underutilized experts without adding a loss term that would hurt model quality. Second, redundant experts — at deployment they replicate the 32 most popular experts onto additional GPUs.

Jenny: That's just hot-shard replication in a sharded database.

Alex: It is exactly hot-shard replication. Same problem, same solution. And there's an EPLB — expert-parallel load balancer — that does placement and dynamic remapping based on observed traffic. Same idea as a query router that's learned which tenant goes to which replica.

Jenny: And the all-to-all itself. You said synchronous.

Alex: Synchronous. That's the killer. Every layer, twice, you do a global shuffle. Every GPU is blocked until every other GPU finishes its part. So your tail latency is set by the slowest expert's GPU. If your routing is even slightly skewed, the popular experts' victims sit idle.

Jenny: It's a global barrier.

Alex: It's two global barriers per layer. 61 layers. That's 122 barriers per decode step. The communication time per layer in DeepSeek's CX7 400Gbps Infiniband setup is around 242 microseconds per layer for the all-to-all. Multiply: 61 times 242 microseconds is about 14.76 milliseconds per token, which caps you at about 67 tokens per second.

Jenny: Per request.

Alex: Per request. That's the theoretical ceiling set just by the all-to-all. On a GB200 NVL72 with 900 gigabytes per second of NVLink instead, the same math gives 820 microseconds per token, or about 1200 tokens per second. Same model, different rack — 18x throughput difference.

Jenny: The interconnect is the model architecture.

Alex: The interconnect is the architecture. That's the whole point of Wide-EP. You're saying "I will design a model that only works if the network is fast enough." And then you go build that network.

Jenny: Okay, LMSYS reproduction.

Alex: LMSYS, May 2025. They published the open-source reproduction of DeepSeek-V3 on commodity H100 — 12 nodes, 96 H100s, using SGLang. Disaggregated prefill and decode. Prefill on 4 nodes — 32 H100s — running TP16 plus EP32 with DP-attention. Decode on the other 9 nodes — 72 H100s — running EP72.

Jenny: Numbers?

Alex: 50,302 prefill tokens per second per node at 4K input length. 22,282 decode tokens per second per node at 2K input length. TTFT between 2 and 5 seconds, ITL around 100 milliseconds. Cost: 20 cents per million output tokens, which is about one-fifth of DeepSeek's own API price.

Jenny: That's the real number. 20 cents per million.

Alex: 20 cents per million on commodity hardware, with open source. Within 5 to 7 percent of DeepSeek's reported numbers on their own H800 stack. The disaggregation story has receipts now.

Jenny: How does KV move between prefill and decode?

Alex: RDMA, scatter-gather, on a background thread. Decode pre-allocates the KV blocks. Prefill writes directly to those blocks over RDMA. Decode reads on its first step. So the prefill-to-decode handoff is just "prefill posts an RDMA write to decode's KV pool, decode wakes up and starts reading."

Jenny: That's exactly RDMA in distributed storage. Pre-allocate, write, read.

Alex: Same primitive. NIXL is the transport. SGLang's HiCache speaks NIXL natively, which is why Dynamo and SGLang compose cleanly — they share the cache transport.

Jenny: And on the EP side? They mention EPLB.

Alex: The LMSYS post is candid about it. EPLB is tuned on in-distribution traffic. Production traffic is not in-distribution. So EPLB is a starting point — you ship with a placement, then you collect observed traffic, then you remap periodically. It's not a finished product. Real deployments need an ongoing balancer.

Jenny: Production system, never done.

Alex: Production system, never done.

Jenny: Okay, third case study. Character.AI.

Alex: Character.AI is the most aggressive cost-side optimization that's been publicly disclosed. They claim about 20,000 QPS sustained — which is something like 20 percent of Google Search's request volume — at under one cent per hour of conversation. They report 33x cost reduction since their 2022 launch and 13.5x cheaper than competitors using leading commercial APIs.

Jenny: How?

Alex: A stack of five compounding techniques, all aimed at KV. Roughly 20x cumulative KV reduction with no measurable quality regression on their internal evals. First — MQA everywhere, not GQA. Eight times reduction versus the standard GQA-8 baseline. They took the quality hit and tuned around it.

Jenny: They went all the way to MQA. That's an architectural decision.

Alex: Architectural. Made at training time. Second — cross-layer KV sharing. Tie the KV across neighboring attention layers, so two adjacent layers literally share the same K and V buffers. Another 2 to 3x reduction.

Jenny: That's like memoizing across layers.

Alex: Exactly. They're betting that neighboring layers don't need totally independent KV — that the information overlap is large enough that sharing is fine. And on their workload, with their evals, that holds. Third — hybrid attention horizons. Interleave local sliding-window attention with sparse global layers. Most layers only attend within a fixed window — say 1024 tokens — and only a few layers do full-context attention. So the cost of long context drops from quadratic in sequence length to linear on the local layers, and only the global layers contribute to KV across the full context.

Jenny: That's like a tiered index. Most reads hit the local index, occasional reads go to the global one.

Alex: Same shape. You're paying full cost only when you have to. Fourth — native int8 training. Weights, activations, and KV all in int8 from training onward, with custom int8 matmul and attention kernels. Eliminates the train-serve dtype mismatch.

Jenny: That's a big bet. Most people train in bf16 and quantize after.

Alex: Most people do, and most people pay a small quality cost on quantization. Character.AI's bet is "if I train native int8, the model learns to live in int8 representation space, and there's no quantization gap." They claim it works. Two times memory reduction, faster matmuls.

Jenny: And fifth?

Alex: Inter-turn caching. KV for every prefilled prefix and every generated message is held in host DRAM, fetched back into HBM on follow-up turns. So multi-turn conversation, which is their entire product, basically eliminates prefill cost in the common case.

Jenny: That's a tiered buffer pool for KV.

Alex: It is exactly a tiered buffer pool. HBM is hot, host DRAM is warm. They could extend to NVMe or RDMA-attached storage for cold — that's what HiCache does in SGLang. Same pattern as a database page cache.

Jenny: And the compounding works out to 20x.

Alex: 8x from MQA, 2-3x from cross-layer sharing, the hybrid layer factor on top of that, 2x from int8, plus the multi-turn savings on top. Compounding gets you to roughly 20x KV reduction at the headline level. Which means batch size goes up roughly 20x at the same memory budget, which means throughput per GPU goes up roughly 20x. That's the entire cost story.

Jenny: Three companies, three different corners of the same matrix.

Alex: Three different corners. DeepSeek's corner is "frontier quality at minimum FLOPs" — they cared about training cost too, because they were the ones training. MLA, FP8, MTP. LMSYS reproduced the same architecture on commodity hardware to prove disaggregation works in public. Character.AI's corner is "cheapest possible consumer chat" — extreme KV reduction, accepting some long-context quality loss because their product is short turns.

Jenny: Different SLOs, different points on the Pareto.

Alex: Different SLOs, different points. None of these recipes are wrong. They're each optimal for what they're trying to do. That's the entire frame.

Jenny: Okay. I want the workflow. If I'm an engineering manager and I get told "stand up serving for this new model," what do I actually do?

Alex: Nine steps. The Scaling Book and the surrounding literature converge on roughly the same sequence. Step 1: write down the SLO contract. TTFT p50 and p99. TBT p50 and p99. Or end-to-end latency if it's non-streaming. Target peak throughput, requests per second or tokens per second. Cost ceiling — dollars per million tokens or dollars per request. And a quality bar — a specific eval suite at a specific threshold.

Jenny: No numbers, no decisions.

Alex: No numbers, no decisions. Stop and get them first. I've seen so many teams skip this and end up six months in optimizing for the wrong thing. Step 2: characterize the workload. Input length distribution, median and p95. Output length distribution. Concurrency profile — steady, diurnal, bursty. Multi-turn or single-shot. Shared system prompt or not. Quality-sensitive subset that needs priority.

Jenny: Because each of those decides a different knob.

Alex: Each maps to a knob. Multi-turn turns on inter-turn caching. Shared system prompt turns on prefix caching. Bursty turns on disaggregation. Mismatched input-to-output ratio decides your prefill-to-decode server ratio — there's a rule of thumb in the Scaling Book of about 3 prefill servers per decode server at 8K input and 512 output for Llama-70B.

Jenny: Workload-dependent.

Alex: Always workload-dependent. Step 3: pick the model. Smallest one that clears your quality bar — on your eval, not benchmarks. Benchmarks lie. Eval on your actual workload. Prefer architectures with cheap KV: GQA-8 as the minimum, MLA if cache will be tight, MQA if it's the bottleneck and you can take the quality hit. Note the quantization-friendliness — model families differ a lot on how well they take FP8 and INT4.

Jenny: Step 4 is the napkin math.

Alex: Step 4 is roofline on paper. Compute KV per token from the formula. Compute param footprint at your chosen quant. Look up B-crit on your target chip. Decide which regime you're going to live in — cache-limited, bandwidth-limited, compute-limited. If the math says it doesn't work — your KV pool fits one user at target context — stop. No amount of tuning will save you. Pick a smaller model, a bigger box, or a better attention variant.

Jenny: That's the move that prevents the six-month detour.

Alex: That is the move that prevents the six-month detour. Step 5: pick the topology. Smallest box where weights plus KV at target batch plus activation headroom fits. TP first, only within an NVLink island. PP only when it genuinely doesn't fit in one node. EP only for MoE and only when batch is big enough that each expert sees at least B-crit over k tokens.

Jenny: And the disaggregation decision.

Alex: Disaggregate if step 2 showed mismatched input and output lengths, or if you have any TTFT pressure. Step 6: pick the engine and a baseline config. vLLM for fast iteration and broad model support. TensorRT-LLM when you need every last percent on a fixed deployment. Set `gpu_memory_utilization` to 0.90 and defaults everywhere else. Do not tune yet.

Jenny: Hands off the knobs.

Alex: Hands off the knobs. Step 7: measure against SLO. TTFT, TBT, throughput, success rate, GPU utilization, HBM bandwidth utilization. Identify the single binding constraint. There's only ever one binding constraint at a time. Tuning a non-binding one is pointless.

Jenny: How do I diagnose memory-bound vs compute-bound at runtime?

Alex: Rule of thumb from nvidia-smi. High SM utilization, above 80%, at moderate memory utilization — compute-bound. Push quantization, push FP8. Low SM utilization, 30 to 50%, with high HBM bandwidth utilization — memory-bound. Push batch, push KV reduction.

Jenny: That's elegant. Two regimes, two readings, two responses.

Alex: Same diagnostic that works for any storage system. Step 8: apply one knob at a time. Symptom to first knob. TBT crushed when prompts arrive — enable chunked prefill, lower `long_prefill_token_threshold`. Repeated prompt prefix in workload — turn on prefix caching. TTFT spiky under load — disaggregate prefill from decode. Throughput plateau below B-crit — raise `max_num_seqs` and `max_num_batched_tokens` together. Quality OK but cost too high — quantize weights to FP8 or INT8, then quantize the KV. ITL still tight after dense tuning — speculative decoding. Long context dominates KV — sliding window or cross-layer sharing. MoE expert under-utilized — raise effective batch via EP or DP-attention.

Jenny: Each one is a single Pareto move.

Alex: Each one is a single Pareto move. Each one slides you along the frontier without expanding it. To expand the frontier you have to go back to step 3 — change the model, the hardware, or the topology.

Jenny: And step 9 is re-measure.

Alex: Re-measure. Stop when SLO is met or when the marginal gain is less than the complexity cost. That last clause is doing a lot of work. Don't keep tuning past good-enough — you'll re-introduce risk for ten percent throughput you didn't need.

Jenny: TRT-LLM grid search. You mentioned it.

Alex: TRT-LLM has a published methodology — six sequential steps. One variable at a time. Baseline with all defaults. Then build flags — quantization choice, kernel fusion choices. Then `max_batch_size` and `max_num_tokens` — set memory ceilings, sweep until the KV pool fills. NVIDIA's reference numbers on Llama-3.3 70B with 4 H100s: optimal at 512 batch size and 2048 num tokens, gives 2474 tokens per second, 21% over the default 2048-token baseline.

Jenny: 21% from two knobs.

Alex: 21% from two knobs, in the right order. Then sharding — TP first inside the NVLink island, PP only when needed. Then runtime knobs — `kv_cache_free_gpu_mem_fraction` around 0.9, in-flight batching, chunked context. Then speculative decoding last. After the dense path is tuned.

Jenny: Why speculative decoding last?

Alex: Because spec decode interacts with everything. It changes your effective batch shape, it changes your kernel selection, it changes your memory usage. If you turn it on early, you can't tell whether your subsequent knob changes are helping the base model or just helping the drafter. Tune the dense path first, get a stable baseline, then add speculative on top.

Jenny: That's good discipline.

Alex: That's the entire point of "one knob at a time." You can't attribute wins if you change three things.

Jenny: Okay, last segment. What do I actually say in an interview, or to my VP, when somebody asks me "how would you build NVIDIA's NIM Factory" or "how would you productize this"?

Alex: This is the editorial extension. The cited sources don't describe NIM internals. But the framework above lets you reason about it. If you walk an interviewer through three-axis Pareto, roofline plus B-crit, KV as the binding constraint, prefill-decode disaggregation, the MLA-GQA-MQA Pareto, and the nine-step workflow — the implicit conclusion is that productizing an LLM serving stack is a roofline-aware grid search across a big matrix.

Jenny: What's the matrix?

Alex: Model times hardware times parallelism times precision times KV strategy times batch shape. Each cell in that matrix is a candidate deployment. NIM Factory, in this framing, is the build pipeline that ships pre-compiled TRT-LLM engine artifacts for some carefully selected subset of those cells.

Jenny: So the hard problem isn't the build, it's deciding which cells to ship.

Alex: That's the entire engineering challenge. Combinatorially, the matrix is huge. You can't validate every cell. So you have to prune intelligently — which models, which hardware tiers, which precisions, which parallelisms — and then validate that the cells you ship perform within tolerance of their predicted rooflines. And keep up with TRT-LLM's release cadence, which is fast.

Jenny: Pre-compiled engines versus customer-compiled. What's the tradeoff?

Alex: Pre-compiled gives you fast cold start, predictable performance, fixed validation surface — your customers see exactly the perf you measured. The cost is inflexibility — if a customer wants a shape or a precision outside the matrix, they can't use the pre-compiled engine. Customer-compiled is the opposite — total flexibility, but they're tracking TRT-LLM's release cadence themselves and they have to do calibration and tuning, which is weeks of work.

Jenny: So NIM Factory is the curated set, and customer-compiled is the long tail.

Alex: That's the right read. And the parallel to your auto scaling world is — pre-compiled NIMs are like AMIs. They're snapshotted, validated, version-pinned artifacts. Customer-compiled is "bring your own image." Both are valid; they serve different customers.

Jenny: The validation matrix. What does that look like at scale?

Alex: It's the combinatorial product of the cells, times the number of perf metrics you care about — TTFT, TBT, throughput at multiple ISL-OSL points, p99 across all of them, plus quality regressions on your eval suite. So it's not just "does this engine run" — it's "does this engine hit its predicted operating point on all of these metrics, on this exact hardware, with this exact driver and CUDA version."

Jenny: Hardware-driver-CUDA matrix on top of model-precision-parallelism matrix.

Alex: That's the real combinatorial explosion. And it's why pre-compiled artifacts are valuable — once a cell is validated, customers don't have to redo that work. The validation is amortized across the install base.

Jenny: What about the other engines? You keep saying vLLM, SGLang, TRT-LLM. Why are there three?

Alex: They occupy different niches in the same matrix. TRT-LLM is the peak-throughput, AOT-compiled, NVIDIA-only path — best when you have a stable model and you'll deploy it at scale. vLLM is the general-purpose JIT path — best for fast iteration, broad model support, when you don't know exactly what you're deploying yet. SGLang is the prefix-heavy, structured, agentic path — RadixAttention is its key primitive, which is content-addressable KV sharing across requests, exposed at a higher level than PagedAttention.

Jenny: RadixAttention versus PagedAttention. What's the difference?

Alex: PagedAttention is virtual memory paging within one process — vLLM's KV manager hands out pages from a free list, per-request. RadixAttention is a shared filesystem cache across processes — a content-addressable index that says "this exact prefix is already cached, here's the pointer, share it." So in SGLang, if two requests share a prefix, they share the actual KV blocks, not just at the page level but at the prefix tree level.

Jenny: That's the same content-addressable trick the Anthropic and OpenAI prompt caching APIs are exposing.

Alex: Same primitive shape. The API-tier prompt caching that the model vendors expose is a productized version of RadixAttention's open-source primitive. Hash the prefix, look up the cached KV, charge the customer less if it hits.

Jenny: And SGLang's other tricks?

Alex: Three more worth knowing. The SGL-Router is a sticky load balancer in front of a fleet — sticky on prefix, so requests that share a prompt get routed to the same replica that's already cached it. Same idea as session-affinity load balancing, just keyed on prefix hash instead of user ID.

Jenny: Sticky sessions for KV.

Alex: Sticky sessions for KV. The overlapped CPU scheduler — they pipeline the dispatcher itself, so while the GPU is running batch N the CPU is building batch N+1. Hides the dispatch overhead the way a CPU pipeline hides the fetch stage.

Jenny: That's the kind of thing you only notice when your dispatch overhead is a real fraction of your step time.

Alex: Which it is, on very fast decode kernels. And HiCache, which we mentioned — tiered buffer pool for KV state across HBM, host DRAM, and RDMA-attached storage. Speaks NIXL, composes with Dynamo.

Jenny: So the three-engine matrix really is workload-shape-driven.

Alex: It really is. NVIDIA ships all three under NIM precisely because no single engine wins everywhere. TRT-LLM for peak throughput on validated cells, vLLM for general-purpose flexibility, SGLang for prefix-heavy and structured and agentic workloads. Pick the engine that matches your workload shape.

Jenny: TensorRT-LLM build step. You said AOT versus JIT.

Alex: TRT-LLM is ahead-of-time compile. You take the model checkpoint, you specify the shapes and the precision and the hardware, and you build a serialized engine artifact. That engine has kernel selection baked in, quantization calibration baked in, graph rewrites baked in. It's a statically-linked C++ binary. The PyExecutor runtime loads it and runs it — Scheduler, KVCacheManager, ModelEngine, Sampler.

Jenny: vLLM is the Python script with the interpreter.

Alex: vLLM is much more dynamic. It picks kernels at runtime, it adapts to shapes on the fly. So vLLM is more flexible and slower to peak. TRT-LLM is less flexible and faster at peak. Classic AOT-versus-JIT tradeoff.

Jenny: And CUDA Graphs?

Alex: CUDA Graph capture is recording a kernel-launch DAG once and replaying it per iteration. Like batching API calls to skip per-call overhead. The hard part is that batch sizes vary at runtime — so TRT-LLM uses CUDA Graph padding to round the dynamic batch up to a captured size, so the recording is reusable.

Jenny: That's exactly the same trick as bucketed batching in a query planner.

Alex: Same trick. Round to a bucket so you can reuse a plan. And then there's PDL — Programmatic Dependent Launch — which is device-level fine-grained pipelining. Kernel B starts as soon as A produces enough output, without waiting for A to fully finish. Even finer than CUDA Graph overlap.

Jenny: Last thing. Speculative decoding family.

Alex: Six drafters worth knowing. N-Gram is statistical — use the prompt itself to draft repeating spans. Lookahead is parallel speculation. Medusa adds extra prediction heads. Eagle and Eagle3 are learned draft models. MTP is DeepSeek's training-time multi-token prediction, repurposed at inference. They all attack the same problem — the autoregressive serial chain — and they all give roughly 1.5 to 2x on compatible workloads. Compatible meaning your tokens are predictable enough that draft acceptance is high.

Jenny: And DeepSeek's MTP gets 80 to 90 percent acceptance because the model was literally trained to do it.

Alex: Trained to do it. That's the trick. Training-aware speculative decoding always beats bolt-on speculative decoding, because the drafter and the verifier are aligned by construction.
