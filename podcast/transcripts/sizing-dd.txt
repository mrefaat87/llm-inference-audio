Jenny: Okay, hold on. Define the roofline carefully for me, because I want to make sure I'm not just borrowing intuition from CPU land.

Alex: Fair. Arithmetic intensity is FLOPs per byte loaded from memory. That's the x-axis. Throughput is the y-axis. If your kernel does very little compute per byte loaded — say, streaming a giant KV cache and doing a couple of dot products — you're on the bandwidth-bound side of the roofline. The HBM is the bottleneck. You can have all the FLOPs in the world and they're idle. If your kernel does a lot of compute per byte — a fat matmul — you're on the compute-bound side. The matmul units are saturated, HBM has spare bandwidth.

Jenny: Right, ops-per-IO. Exactly the same plot you'd draw for a storage tier. Big sequential reads versus random small reads.

Jenny: So a hardware number and a precision number.

Jenny: 240. Okay.

Alex: For H100, same source, it's about 280 tokens, bf16. And here's the part that changes everyone's mental model: if you go to int8 weights with bf16 activations, B_crit drops to 120. Half. Because the bytes-per-parameter dropped, so you need fewer FLOPs per byte to be compute-bound.

Jenny: Wait, but if I quantize both weights and activations to int8?

Jenny: That's actually a beautiful little observation. So when somebody says "we switched to FP8" — that's not just a memory savings story, that's a roofline-shift story.

Alex: It is, and Hopper's native FP8 is the reason DeepSeek-V3's whole pipeline pencils out, but we're getting ahead of ourselves.

Jenny: Free until it's not.

Alex: Free until it's not. And then it's strictly worse.

Jenny: Now — prefill versus decode. You said they live on opposite sides. Walk me through that, because in my head they're both just transformer forward passes.

Alex: They're both forward passes, and that's the trap. Prefill processes the whole prompt at once. If the prompt is two thousand tokens, your "batch" — from the matmul's perspective — is two thousand. Massively compute-bound for any reasonable prompt length. The Scaling Book says prefill attention is compute-bound for sequences roughly above 480 tokens, and the linear layers are compute-bound because the prompt itself is the batch.

Jenny: So one request with a long prompt is already past B_crit on its own.

Alex: Already past it. You don't need to batch across requests to be compute-bound during prefill. You're optimizing for MFU — Model FLOPs Utilization — and the right target is "fill the matmul units."

Jenny: And decode?

Alex: Decode is the opposite universe. You're generating one token at a time per sequence. The attention operation per step has arithmetic intensity of approximately one. That's structural. You load the entire KV cache for the sequence, do a tiny dot product with one new query, and that's the step. The Scaling Book is blunt about it — decode attention is always memory-bandwidth-bound. You cannot improve the arithmetic intensity of a single-token attention. It's literally the geometry of the operation.

Jenny: Always. That's a strong word.

Alex: It's structural. The only way to improve it is to batch across sequences — different sequences sharing the weight load. Which is what continuous batching is doing. But the per-sequence work, the KV streaming, scales with batch and you're still bandwidth-bound on the cache.

Jenny: So prefill wants small batch, low TTFT, sweat the compute. Decode wants huge batch, amortize the weight load, sweat the bandwidth.

Alex: Two phases, two regimes, two optimal batch shapes. And the Scaling Book just states the conclusion directly: separate prefill and generation servers. Prefill batch of one, low TTFT, compute-bound. Generate at large batch, high throughput, memory-bound. KV cache transferred over the network between them.

Jenny: Which is exactly what disaggregated serving is. PD split.

Alex: That's the whole architectural argument for PD disaggregation in one sentence. Two roofline regimes, two optimal configurations, you can't satisfy both on one engine without compromising one.

Jenny: Okay. I'm going to push on this. People have been running prefill and decode on the same engine for two years. vLLM does it. It works. So what's the actual quantified cost of mixing them?

Alex: vLLM's V1 scheduler has a specific rule for this — decode-priority. Quoting the anatomy blog: "the V1 engine prioritizes decode requests already in the running queue before processing new prefill requests from waiting." That's the elevator-scheduling answer.

Jenny: Finish what's already moving before letting new requests in.

Alex: Exactly. Because if you let a fresh prefill jump in front of fifty in-flight decodes, every one of those decodes stalls for the duration of that prefill. A two-thousand-token prefill is maybe four hundred milliseconds. You just added four hundred milliseconds of ITL to every other request. Conversely, if you always let decode jump ahead, new prompts never start. The scheduler is constantly trying to find a fair point in between.

Jenny: And chunked prefill is the engine's escape valve for that.

Canonical reference is the Scaling Book.

Jenny: That just inverts my whole intuition. I keep thinking of weights as the heavy thing.

Batch 1: KV is 6.7 gigs, total memory 32.7 gigs, step time 4.98 milliseconds, throughput 200 tokens per second. Batch 16: KV jumps to 107 gigs, total memory 133, step time 20 milliseconds, throughput 788 tokens per second. Jenny: So throughput goes up about five-x from batch 1 to batch 240, but step time goes up fifty-x.

The batch ceiling on this workload is set by KV size, not by compute.

Jenny: So the roofline says "compute-bound at 240" — you'd love to be there — and the KV math says "you cannot get there, you do not have the memory."

Jenny: Seven-x. Per token.

Alex: At a hundred thousand tokens of context, that's a different universe.

Jenny: So what's the actual trick? GQA shares KV heads. What does MLA do?

Alex: MLA compresses K and V jointly into a small latent vector. There's a projection matrix, learned during training, that takes the full K and V and squeezes them into a low-dimensional latent. Only the latent gets cached. When you actually need K and V for an attention computation, you up-project from the latent.

Jenny: So you trade cache size for a tiny extra matmul on every read.

Alex: That's exactly the trade. And it's a great trade because — here's the punchline — you were memory-bandwidth-bound anyway. You had compute to spare. So you spend some of that spare compute on the up-projection, you cut your KV cache by roughly an order of magnitude, and you climb straight up the roofline.

Jenny: That is — okay. That is genuinely elegant. The constraint is bandwidth, you spend the FLOPs you already had to relieve the bandwidth.

Alex: It's the same shape as speculative decoding, just at a different layer of the stack. Spend cheap FLOPs to win against an expensive memory constraint.

Jenny: Wait, isn't that just GQA with extra steps? Sharing K and V?

Alex: It's the same family. The DeepSeek paper situates them on a spectrum. GQA and MQA — multiple query heads share a single set of KV pairs. Coarse-grained sharing. MLA — joint latent compression of K and V across all heads. Fine-grained learned compression. Same intent, different mechanism, and MLA is more aggressive about it.

Jenny: And the quality cost?

Alex: The paper claims minimal — DeepSeek-V3 is competitive with frontier models, and MLA is part of how they got there. The quality cost is buried in the joint training; you have to train with MLA from scratch, you can't bolt it onto a GQA model.

Jenny: That's a deployment constraint right there. You can't retrofit MLA, you ship MLA from day one.

Alex: Which is exactly why the only model in production at scale with MLA is DeepSeek's. It's a co-design decision from training.

Jenny: Okay what about the other techniques on that spectrum? Windowed KV, quantization?

Alex: The paper lists them. Sliding window — keep only a recent window, discard older tokens. The paper is honest about this — quote, "compromises long-context reasoning." Which is the tradeoff most engines hide. If you're doing customer support chats that don't need long memory, fine. If you're doing code review with thirty thousand tokens of context, not fine. Quantized compression — store KV in int8 or lower. The DeepSeek paper says "minimal impact on model performance," and people generally agree at int8. At int4 it depends a lot on the model.

Jenny: And block size? In vLLM, PagedAttention has a block size?

For bf16 that's two bytes. Sixteen is the empirical sweet spot for transformer KV layouts. Smaller blocks waste metadata. Larger blocks waste memory on partial blocks at the tail of each sequence.

Jenny: Right, exactly like a filesystem block size or a virtual memory page. Smaller pages, more page table overhead. Larger pages, more internal fragmentation.

Alex: It is literally a page size choice. And PagedAttention is virtual memory paging inside a single GPU process. Same primitive.

Sequence sharding is impractical because decode is sequential by definition, there's no sequence to shard.

Jenny: So the only sharding that works for decode is model parallelism.

Jenny: Which is why every serious deployment ends up looking the same.

The specific techniques cited in the broader literature — cross-layer KV sharing, sliding-window-plus-global hybrid attention, INT8 KV cache quantization — are not quantified in their cited page. So the 33x is the company's, not a per-technique breakdown the source supports.

Jenny: That's an important honesty note for an interview. The cost number is citeable. The per-technique attribution is not.

Alex: Exactly. The story you tell is: Character.AI made aggressive KV-side architecture choices to serve consumer chat economically, and the cost numbers they publish are credible, and the per-mechanism breakdown is industry inference from the broader literature, not directly attributed in their blog.

Jenny: And DeepSeek is the opposite case study.

Alex: Opposite philosophy. Character.AI took a fixed model class — short turns, very high QPS, consumer chat — and optimized the serving stack against it. DeepSeek-V3 took a fixed cluster — 2,048 H800s, fixed network topology — and redesigned the model to suit the cluster. Hardware-aware co-design.

Jenny: What are the architectural choices?

Alex: Four. One: MLA, already covered. 70 kilobytes per token KV at bf16 versus 516 for Llama 3.1 405B GQA. Two: MoE — DeepSeekMoE — 671 billion total parameters, 37 billion activated per token. The per-token compute cost: 250 gigaFLOPs for DeepSeek-V3, versus 394 gigaFLOPs for Qwen-72B dense, versus 2,448 gigaFLOPs for Llama 405B dense. About ten-x less compute per token for comparable or superior quality.

Jenny: Wait. Ten-x less compute per token than dense Llama 405B.

Alex: Ten-x. That's the entire economic argument for MoE. You activate only the experts you need.

Jenny: Right, expert parallelism is like sharding a database by tenant ID, except the routing key — the gate output — is recomputed every token.

Alex: That's the analogy. And it's a hard sharding problem because the routing is dynamic and skewed. Some experts get hit way more than others. Which is why you need EPLB and redundant experts.

Jenny: The hot-shard problem.

Alex: The hot-shard problem at every token. Three: FP8 mixed-precision training. Quote: "FP8 significantly reduces memory consumption by half compared to BF16, plus reducing communication volume by 50 percent compared to BF16 via FP8 EP dispatch." Both the storage and the wire bytes shrink. Which is exactly what we computed in the all-to-all math — fewer bytes per token in the dispatch, less time on the network.

Jenny: And four?

Alex: MTP. Multi-Token Prediction. We covered it — 80 to 90 percent acceptance on the second token, 1.8x TPS without MTP, up to 2.82x on B200 with three stacked layers.

Jenny: Okay so the side-by-side: Character.AI is consumer-chat economics through aggressive KV reduction. DeepSeek is frontier-reasoning at long context through MLA, MoE, FP8, and MTP. Both publish wild numbers, both are credible in different domains. What's the tradeoff you'd state for each?

Alex: Character.AI almost certainly loses some long-context quality — sliding window is the honest tradeoff. You serve a chat product, the quality cost is mostly invisible to the user. DeepSeek's MLA has the compute cost of up-projection on every read. The way they pay for it is by living in the regime where they had compute to spare anyway.

Jenny: So both are exploiting the asymmetry of decode being bandwidth-bound. Just on different parts of the cache shape.

Alex: That's the unifying observation. Both stacks are "spend compute, save bandwidth" in different forms.

Jenny: Okay. Tuning. The TRT-LLM playbook. Walk me through the grid search.

Alex: TensorRT-LLM has a published Performance Tuning Guide. The case study is Llama 3.3 70B on four H100 SXM 80-gig GPUs, sequence in and out of 2048 each. Two main parameters. max_batch_size, default 2048. max_num_tokens, default 8192. The guide recommends sweeping powers of two.

Jenny: Two parameters, big interaction surface.

Alex: Big interaction surface. The results: at max_batch_size 64, throughput 1,944 tokens per second — bottlenecked, batch too small. At max_batch_size 512, throughput 2,467 — optimal, 21 percent better than the default. At max_batch_size 2048 — the default — throughput 2,044, over-provisioned, wastes memory on KV admin.

Jenny: So the default is worse than 512.

Alex: The default is worse than 512 by a meaningful margin. With max_batch_size fixed at 512, sweep max_num_tokens: 2048 gives 2,474, best. 8192 (the default) gives 2,467. 16,384 gives 2,461.

Jenny: So 21 percent free throughput just from a two-knob grid search.

Alex: Free throughput. And the guide states explicitly: grid search across combinations, don't tune independently. Because the two interact through KV memory accounting. Too small max_num_tokens bottlenecks scheduling. Too large max_num_tokens consumes prompt memory and shrinks the available KV cache space, potentially OOM-ing you.

Jenny: That's the kind of insight that's only obvious in retrospect. The two parameters are coupled through KV memory, so they're not independent knobs.

Alex: They look independent in the config file. They're not. And this is true generally — most engine knobs are coupled through KV memory or through scheduler queue dynamics.

Jenny: What about TRT-LLM versus vLLM as engines? Like, when do you reach for which?

Alex: This is the workload-shape question. TRT-LLM is AOT-compiled — ahead-of-time. You build a TensorRT engine, which is a serialized, pre-optimized artifact with kernel selection, quantization calibration, and graph rewrites baked in. It's loaded by the PyExecutor runtime — the same four-component idea as vLLM, scheduler plus KV cache manager plus model engine plus sampler.

Jenny: Static binary versus Python script.

Alex: Exactly. Statically-linked C++ versus Python with an interpreter. vLLM is JIT, flexible, easy to deploy for new models, but you pay some overhead. TRT-LLM is rigid — engine is shape-specific, hardware-specific, precision-specific — but you get peak throughput. CUDA Graph capture, recording a kernel-launch DAG once and replaying it per iteration, gets fused into the engine. In-flight batching — continuous batching — is also compiled in.

Jenny: So TRT-LLM is "what if you compiled the scheduler decisions into the kernel launches."

Alex: Roughly that. PDL — Programmatic Dependent Launch — goes further. Kernel B starts as soon as A produces enough output, without waiting for A to fully finish. Device-level fine-grained pipelining.

Jenny: Like overlap-scheduling but at the kernel boundary.

Alex: Below the kernel boundary. The MLA optimizations in TRT-LLM specifically — eliminating Q/K/V concat copies, FP8 Context FMHA matched to FP8 KV cache, 4-way LM-head tensor parallel — these are kernel-level surgery driven by DeepSeek R1 as the target workload.

Jenny: So if you're running DeepSeek R1 at scale, TRT-LLM has hand-written kernels for it.

Alex: Yes. And there's a quantization stack underneath: FP8 as the throughput tier on Hopper, NVFP4 as the Blackwell-specific aggressive tier, INT4 AWQ as the small-batch memory-bound escape hatch. Three layers of compression for three different operating points.

Jenny: SGLang would be the third engine in this story, right?

Alex: SGLang's the third. The NIM Factory framing — which is editorial, not from the cited sources — is essentially: TRT-LLM for peak throughput, vLLM for general-purpose, SGLang for prefix-heavy, structured, or agentic workloads. Three engines, three workload shapes.

Jenny: And SGLang's specialness is RadixAttention.

Alex: RadixAttention plus HiCache plus jump-forward decoding for structured outputs. Compressed FSM — when you're generating JSON, large spans are deterministic, the grammar forces them, you can skip the model entirely for those spans. Same idea as a query optimizer constant-folding deterministic predicates.

Jenny: Beautiful. That's just SQL planning ideas applied to token generation.

Alex: Exactly.

Jenny: Okay, decision framework. If someone hands you a workload and asks "size this," walk me through the questions you ask.

Alex: First question: what does the workload look like? Short turns or long context? High QPS or low QPS? Prefix-heavy or prefix-light? Structured output or free-form? Those answers tell you which engine class is most appropriate. Second question: what hardware is available? H100, H800, B200, GB200 NVL72, TPU? Different memory bandwidth, different network topology, different B_crit. Third question: what's the model? Dense or MoE? GQA, MQA, MLA? Native precision support — does it train in FP8 or only in bf16?

Jenny: That's already a six-dimensional matrix.

Alex: Now add precision — bf16, FP8, NVFP4, INT8, INT4. Parallelism — TP degree, EP degree, PP degree, DP degree. KV strategy — paged, radix, tiered, quantized. Batch shape — max_batch_size, max_num_tokens. The combinatorial validation matrix is enormous.

Jenny: This is where NIM Factory comes in.

Alex: This is editorial — none of the cited sources describe NIM Factory's internals — but the framing is: NIM Factory is the answer to "pre-compile and validate the cells of that matrix you care about, so customers don't have to." Pre-compiled TRT-LLM engine artifacts shipped per model-times-precision-times-hardware-times-parallelism cell. The build pipeline's hard problem is not the build, it's the validation.

Jenny: Pre-compiled versus customer-compiled is fast cold start and predictable performance, at the cost of inflexibility on shapes outside the matrix.

Alex: Exactly. Customer compile gives flexibility but costs you weeks of build and calibration time and the burden of tracking TRT-LLM's release cadence. Pre-compile gives a fixed validation surface — fast, predictable, but inflexible.

Jenny: So how do you actually pick the cells you ship?

Alex: That's the editorial extension. The cited sources don't tell you how to prune the matrix. What they do tell you is the framework for thinking about which cells matter. The right operating regime depends on B_crit and KV size. Prefill and decode want opposite batch shapes — disaggregation exists for that reason. MLA-class KV compression buys an order of magnitude at long context. The public deployments that hit best-in-class economics — DeepSeek's MoE-FP8-MTP stack, Character.AI's KV-radical stack, LMSYS's reproduction — all live in different corners of the matrix.

Jenny: Which is to say there isn't one best cell. There are several local optima for different workload shapes.

Alex: Several local optima. The job of a productization team is to choose which optima to ship and which to leave behind.

Jenny: Okay, the interview question. If someone asks you in an interview "how would you size an LLM serving deployment?" — what do you actually say?

Alex: I'd say: I'd start with the roofline. I'd compute B_crit for the target hardware and precision. I'd estimate the per-token KV cache from the model's architecture. I'd identify which phase — prefill or decode — has the tightest SLO, and decide whether to disaggregate. I'd pick a parallelism strategy based on whether the model is dense or MoE and whether the network can carry the all-to-all. I'd grid-search the batch knobs against a representative workload. I'd benchmark and iterate.

Jenny: And the honest tradeoffs.

Alex: The honest tradeoffs are: every KV-reduction technique has a quality cost, mostly hidden. Sliding window costs long-context. Aggressive quantization costs marginal quality. MLA costs training-time complexity and a small inference compute overhead. Disaggregation costs operational complexity — you now have two pools to manage and KV transfer over RDMA between them. Speculative decoding costs accuracy on the tail, even when acceptance is high on average. There is no free win at scale.

Jenny: No free wins.

Alex: No free wins. Just better-shaped tradeoffs for your specific workload.
