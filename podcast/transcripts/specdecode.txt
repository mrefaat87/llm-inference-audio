Alex: So I want to start with a number that should make your auto-scaling brain itch. On a 70B model, a single decode step takes maybe 20 to 50 milliseconds. The GPU during that time? It's busy. HBM is pegged. But the actual arithmetic units — the tensor cores — are mostly sitting there waiting.

Jenny: Wait, hold on. Pegged HBM but idle compute? That's the GPU equivalent of an EC2 instance where the NIC is saturated but the CPU is at 5%. That's a provisioning failure.

Alex: It's exactly that. And it's not a bug, it's the fundamental shape of decode. Every single token you generate, you have to drag all 140 gigs of model weights from HBM into the compute units to produce one token. The math itself is trivial — it's a matrix-vector multiply. The data movement is the whole job.

Jenny: So decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. The tensor cores are like a warehouse with a tiny loading dock — doesn't matter how fast the forklifts inside are.

Alex: Perfect framing. And here's the question that launches this whole topic: if I've already paid the cost of dragging those weights across HBM, can I get more than one token out of that trip?

Jenny: Oh. Oh, that's a good question. Because you're paying the bandwidth tax anyway.

Alex: Right. The forward pass over one token versus the forward pass over, say, four tokens — the weight loading cost is basically identical. The arithmetic goes up, but we already established arithmetic is the underutilized resource. So in principle, processing four tokens at once should cost about the same wall-clock time as processing one.

Jenny: But you can't just generate four tokens at once. Each one depends on the previous. That's the whole reason decode is sequential in the first place.

Alex: Exactly the right objection. You can't generate them in parallel. But you can verify them in parallel. And that's the trick.

Jenny: Okay, unpack that.

Alex: Imagine I have a small, fast model — call it 1 billion parameters instead of 70. It's cheap. Each decode step on it takes maybe a millisecond. I let that small model speed-run ahead and propose four candidate tokens. Then I take those four proposed tokens and feed them to the big 70B model all at once, in a single forward pass — same shape as prefill.

Jenny: Like a prefill over the proposed continuation.

Alex: Exactly like that. And at each of the four positions, the big model says: "given everything before this, what would I have predicted? Does my distribution agree with the proposal?" If yes, accept. If no, reject and substitute the big model's preferred token at that position.

Jenny: So this is optimistic concurrency control. The draft model is the optimistic write, the verifier is the validation pass, and you commit the prefix that validates.

Alex: That's the cleanest description I've heard. It's literally optimistic concurrency. And the speedup math is beautiful — if the draft proposes four tokens and the verifier accepts three of them and corrects the fourth, you've advanced four tokens in roughly the time of one big-model decode step. That's a 3 to 4x throughput multiplier on decode.

Jenny: Hang on, hang on. I want to push on something. You said "if the verifier disagrees, reject and substitute." That sounds like you're approximating. Like you're trading some quality for the speedup.

Alex: This is the part that surprises everyone. The output distribution is mathematically identical to running the big model alone. It's not an approximation. There's a rejection sampling scheme that proves it — when you reject and resample correctly, the final distribution you sample from is exactly the verifier's distribution.

Jenny: So this is a free lunch.

Alex: It's the closest thing to a free lunch in this whole field. There's no quality-throughput tradeoff. The costs are operational — you're running two models, holding both in GPU memory, and your serving stack is more complex.

Jenny: Okay so the obvious question — what determines whether this actually wins? Because clearly if the draft is wrong all the time, you're just doing extra work for nothing.

Alex: Acceptance rate is everything. At 90% acceptance with four draft tokens, you're getting roughly three and a half tokens per big-model step. That's a 3x speedup. At 70%, you're around 2.4 tokens per step, so 2x. At 50%, you're getting maybe 1.7 tokens, and the overhead of running the draft eats most of the win. Below 40%, you're underwater. You'd be faster just decoding normally.

Jenny: So what governs acceptance rate? Is it the draft model's quality?

Alex: It's how well the draft model's distribution matches the verifier's distribution on the specific text being generated. Code, templates, repetitive structured output — the draft nails it, you get 90%+ acceptance. Highly creative or domain-specific text — the draft and verifier diverge, acceptance tanks.

Jenny: So this is workload-dependent. The same deployment might be a 3x win on a code-completion endpoint and a wash on a creative-writing endpoint.

Alex: Exactly. Which is why the algorithm taxonomy matters — there are now five different ways to source the draft tokens, and they make different tradeoffs.

Jenny: Walk me through them. What are the choices?

Alex: The classical one is draft-target — you pick a smaller model from the same family, say Llama 3 8B drafting for Llama 3 70B. Acceptance is decent, 60 to 80%. But you're now holding two models in memory. 70B in FP16 is 140 gigs, plus 14 more for the draft. That's already multi-GPU territory with less headroom.

Jenny: So you pay a real memory tax.

Alex: Real memory tax. Then there's Medusa, which says: forget a separate model, let me graft extra prediction heads onto the target model itself. Each head predicts one future token. Tiny memory overhead, like 1 to 5% extra parameters, but you have to fine-tune the target.

Jenny: That's nice because you're not maintaining a second deployment artifact.

Alex: Right. Then EAGLE — this is the current production default in vLLM, SGLang, TRT-LLM. It uses a small auxiliary network, maybe 0.3B parameters for a 70B target, but the clever part is it conditions on the verifier's hidden states, not just its tokens. So the draft is genuinely close to the verifier's internal representation.

Jenny: Wait, that's interesting. By conditioning on hidden states it's essentially cheating — it has more information than a standalone draft model would have.

Alex: That's exactly why it works. Acceptance rates of 70 to 90%, with a tiny memory footprint. EAGLE has basically displaced classical draft-target.

Jenny: What if I can't fine-tune? Say I'm serving a vendor model and I don't own the weights.

Alex: Then training-free methods. N-gram speculation is the simplest — you build a dictionary from the prefill tokens, and when the model emits a sequence you've seen before, you propose whatever followed it last time. Zero parameters, zero training.

Jenny: That sounds embarrassingly simple. Does it actually work?

Alex: On code and RAG, it's incredible. Documents have repetition, code has patterns. The acceptance rate can be very high in those domains. On novel prose, it falls apart. Then there's Lookahead Decoding which generates n-grams on the fly using a Jacobi iteration — also training-free, also drop-in.

Jenny: Okay, one last pushback. You said earlier that decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and that's why we have spare compute. Does that still hold at high batch size?

Alex: Caught it. No, it does not. Once you batch enough requests together, decode becomes compute-bound — the spare arithmetic capacity is consumed by other requests in the batch. Speculative decoding loses its leverage at very high batch sizes. Production systems often disable it above some threshold.

Jenny: So spec decode is most valuable when you have low concurrency and long outputs. Single-tenant inference, latency-sensitive workloads, code completion.

Alex: That's the sweet spot. And in batched serving, the variable acceptance across requests is its own challenge — request A might accept 90%, request B accepts 30% in the same batch. The system has to do per-request rollback. Research suggests you need average acceptance above 60 to 70% across the batch to even break even.

Jenny: So this isn't just an algorithm, it's a serving-system feature.

Alex: It's deeply a serving-system feature. Which is a good place to look forward.

Jenny: Where does this take us next?

Alex: Everything we've talked about — paged attention, continuous batching, quantization, speculative decoding — these are single-GPU or single-node tricks. They squeeze the most out of one box. But the frontier models don't fit on one box. A 405B model, a trillion-parameter MoE — those need to be split across many GPUs, sometimes many nodes. And the moment you split a model, you've created a distributed system problem on top of an inference problem.

Jenny: Oh. So now the bottleneck isn't HBM bandwidth, it's the interconnect.

Alex: Exactly. The next concepts are about parallelism strategies — tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, expert parallelism — and how you choose between them based on your network topology and your batch size. It's the same kind of bin-packing decision you make in capacity planning, but the dimensions are weirder.

Jenny: I'm already thinking about it as a placement problem. Which makes me suspect everything I know about NUMA and rack-locality is going to come roaring back.

Alex: It absolutely does. That's where we go next.
