Alex: So here's the thing I want to start with. If you already know vLLM well — you understand PagedAttention, you understand continuous batching, you've probably tuned a few production deployments — then TensorRT-LLM is going to feel familiar at the primitive level and completely alien at the workflow level. Same paging. Same continuous batching. Same prefix sharing. But the way you actually get a model serving is on the other side of a wall.

Jenny: A wall meaning what, exactly? Because when I hear "TensorRT-LLM" I think "vLLM but NVIDIA wrote it," and I'm guessing that's wrong.

Alex: That's wrong, yeah. The cleanest way I've found to frame it — and the cited sources actually back this up — is AOT versus JIT. vLLM is JIT-compiled like Python. You ship a model checkpoint, the engine inspects it, allocates pages, picks attention kernels at startup, and starts serving. TensorRT-LLM is closer to AOT-compiled like C++. There's a meaningful build step where the engine for your specific model, your specific hardware, your specific max batch size, and your specific precision is produced. And then you serve that artifact.

Jenny: Wait, hold on. So when you say "build step" you mean an actual compile step that produces a binary blob?

Alex: Pretty much. The docs hub literally says the API is used to "build NVIDIA TensorRT engines that contain state-of-the-art optimizations." That's the identity of the project in one line. TensorRT-LLM is the LLM-shaped front door to the underlying TensorRT compiler stack. NVIDIA has had TensorRT for years — it's a graph-level compiler that does aggressive kernel fusion, quantization calibration, layer selection, precision lowering, and emits a serialized engine that gets replayed at inference. TensorRT-LLM inherits that build-then-run pattern.

Jenny: Okay, so the analogy I want to pin down — vLLM is "Python interpreter loads your script and runs it," TensorRT-LLM is "you statically link a C++ binary for this exact CPU and ship that binary." Is that fair?

Alex: That's exactly fair. And the introl optimization guide gives you the operational consequence in one bullet: "Setup Complexity: TensorRT-LLM High (weeks); vLLM Low (hours)." Weeks versus hours. That's the price you pay for AOT.

Jenny: Weeks. For one model.

Alex: For one model on one hardware target at one precision and one max-shape configuration. If you change any of those — different GPU, different precision, different max sequence length — you might be rebuilding.

Jenny: Okay, so why would anyone do that? What do you get on the other side?

Alex: You get the kernel selection and the graph rewrites and the quantization baked into the artifact you serve. No interpreter overhead. No "let me figure out which attention kernel to use at runtime." It's all decided. And the runtime that loads the engine is small and tight. The architecture overview describes it as a PyExecutor with four explicit subcomponents. There's a Scheduler that determines which requests execute at each step. A KVCacheManager that handles KV cache allocation. A ModelEngine that loads and executes the model on GPU. And a Sampler that applies sampling strategies — greedy, top-k, top-p, beam search — to the logits.

Jenny: That's the same four-pillar shape vLLM has, basically. Scheduler, paged KV cache, model runner, sampler.

Alex: It's the same four pillars. Which is why I said the primitives feel familiar. The difference is that in TensorRT-LLM, the ModelEngine isn't running a PyTorch graph that gets dispatched op by op. It's running a compiled engine where most of the dispatch decisions have been resolved at build time.

Jenny: Got it. So memorize that four-component split because everything we talk about plugs into one of those four.

Alex: Exactly. CUDA Graph capture — that plugs into the Scheduler and ModelEngine boundary. Paged attention — KVCacheManager. Speculative decoding — Sampler plus ModelEngine. Wide expert parallelism — ModelEngine plus a redesigned communication layer. Every optimization we'll talk about today lives in one of those four boxes.

Jenny: Okay, before we go deeper — give me the ecosystem context. Who's using this? Why does it matter that we know it?

Alex: The developer hub names AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft, and Grammarly among ecosystem partners. The canonical deployment targets are the models you'd expect — Llama 3.3 70B, Llama 4 Scout 17B, DeepSeek R1, Nemotron v3 Super, GPT-OSS, Qwen3, Kimi K2 Thinking. The release cadence is heavy. The GitHub repo shows 76 total releases with v1.2.1 dated April 20, 2026. So this is not a static project. It's moving fast.

Jenny: 76 releases. That's a lot of churn for something you're supposed to AOT compile against.

Alex: And that tension is real. Hold that thought, because it's going to matter a lot when we get to the productization story in segment five. The whole point of NIM is to absorb that churn for you.

Jenny: Right. Okay. Vendor numbers — what's NVIDIA claiming?

Alex: The marketing claim attached to the v1.0 cut is "8x AI inference performance improvement." And on Blackwell Ultra, "up to 50x better performance and 35x lower cost," specifically for agentic AI. Those are vendor numbers, not third-party benchmarks. Always keep that qualifier.

Jenny: Always. Especially "50x." That's the kind of number where you want to know what's in the denominator.

Alex: Right. And we'll get to specific operating points later that are at least somewhat reproducible. But yeah — when you see "50x" in a slide deck, your first question is "compared to what configuration on what hardware running what workload."

Jenny: Okay so let's go deeper. We have the four-component runtime, we have the AOT build model, we have the basic identity. What's actually in the optimization stack?

Alex: So this is segment two and it's where the listener should leave with a real mental model. The optimizations TRT-LLM ships fall into three families. Ones that attack CPU-side launch overhead. Ones that attack memory bandwidth or memory footprint. And ones that attack arithmetic by reshaping how decode steps are computed.

Jenny: Three buckets. Okay. Start with CPU.

Alex: CPU first. The big one is CUDA Graphs. The architecture overview names CUDA Graph as a first-class runtime feature that "reduces CPU overhead by capturing kernel sequences." The mechanism is exactly what it sounds like. The host records the sequence of kernel launches that make up one decode iteration. Then it replays the captured graph in a single submission instead of issuing N individual launches.

Jenny: So this is — wait, this is basically batching the API calls between CPU and GPU?

Alex: That's a perfect way to say it. Every kernel launch from CPU to GPU has overhead. Setting up the launch parameters, the driver round trip, all of it. If your model is small, or your GPU is fast enough that per-kernel work starts to rival per-kernel launch latency, the CPU becomes the gate. CUDA Graphs let you record the launch DAG once and replay it.

Jenny: Why didn't you have to do this in 2018?

Alex: Because GPUs got faster. A100 to H100 to B200 — the math gets faster every generation. The launch overhead doesn't scale down. So the relative cost of host dispatch keeps growing. At some point, your decode iteration is doing tens of microseconds of math and tens of microseconds of launch overhead, and now half your wall clock is the CPU.

Jenny: That's the classic "your bottleneck moves" story. You optimize the math and now the dispatch is the bottleneck. Okay so CUDA Graphs solves that. But isn't the catch that you can only capture a static shape? What happens when your batch size changes between iterations?

Alex: That's exactly the catch and it's why CUDA Graph padding exists. The overview says CUDA Graph padding yields "up to a 22% end-to-end throughput increase." Padding here means rounding the dynamic batch up to a captured size so the graph can be reused. If you've captured graphs for batch sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and your current batch is 13, you pad to 16 and replay the size-16 graph. The extra slots compute on garbage that you discard.

Jenny: So you're paying a little extra compute to skip a lot of CPU overhead.

Alex: Right. And that tradeoff is worth it almost every time on modern hardware. The 22% number is end-to-end. So that's not a microbenchmark, that's a real workload.

Jenny: Okay. What's the related knob? You mentioned an overlap scheduler.

Alex: The Overlap Scheduler. The architecture overview describes it as a runtime feature that hides CPU-bound latency behind GPU computation. The mechanism is the same idea you'd see in a CPU pipeline — run the scheduler bookkeeping for iteration N+1 while the GPU is still computing iteration N.

Jenny: That's pipelining the dispatcher. Build batch N+1 while the GPU runs batch N. Like a CPU pipeline hiding the fetch stage.

Alex: That's the cleanest framing. CUDA Graph capture handles the static-shape kernel-launch overhead. The Overlap Scheduler handles the dynamic batch-construction overhead. Together they basically hide most CPU-side cost behind GPU compute.

Jenny: Okay, second bucket. Memory bandwidth.

Alex: Memory bandwidth. Two big mechanisms. Kernel fusion and paged KV with custom attention kernels. Introl describes the fusion strategy as combining "multiple transformer operations into single optimized CUDA kernels. LayerNorm, matrix multiplications, bias additions, and activation functions execute together."

Jenny: Why is that a memory bandwidth win and not a compute win?

Alex: Because the bottleneck on a modern GPU isn't the math. The bottleneck is reading and writing the activation tensors to HBM between every op. The unfused chain — LayerNorm reads from HBM, writes to HBM. Matmul reads from HBM, writes to HBM. Bias add reads from HBM, writes to HBM. Activation reads from HBM, writes to HBM. Every one of those round trips costs more than the actual arithmetic on a Hopper or a Blackwell.

Jenny: Wait, more than the arithmetic?

Alex: More than the arithmetic, in many cases. Modern GPUs are absurdly fast at FP16 and FP8 matmul — Tensor Cores eat that work for breakfast. The memory hierarchy is the gate. Fusing LayerNorm plus matmul plus bias plus activation into one kernel means the intermediates stay in registers or shared memory and never touch HBM at all.

Jenny: So you're treating the GPU's L1 and L2 and registers like a CPU cache hierarchy and trying to keep the working set in the fast tier.

Alex: Exactly that. And the fetch.ai writeup adds another framing — these fused kernels are wrapped as TensorRT plugins. The GEMM plugin, the GPT-attention plugin. So they slot into the compiled graph as composable building blocks.

Jenny: Okay. Paged attention. I assume this is the same paged attention I know from vLLM.

Alex: Same primitive. Introl describes it as allocating KV cache in non-contiguous blocks rather than requiring continuous memory regions, with block-level allocation enabling sharing among requests with common prefixes. The semantics are the ones you already know — block-level allocation, no fragmentation, prefix sharing.

Jenny: So what's different?

Alex: The kernels. Introl notes that hand-optimized implementations of multi-head and grouped-query attention leverage Tensor Core instructions. FP8 attention kernels on Hopper and Blackwell GPUs provide additional speedups. So vLLM has paging plus a generic kernel. TRT-LLM has paging plus kernels that have been hand-tuned for specific GPU architectures and specific precisions.

Jenny: That's the AOT story showing up again. You can hand-tune a kernel for a specific shape and precision because you committed to that shape and precision at build time.

Alex: That's exactly right. And you see the payoff in the release notes — there's a feature called Multiblock Attention that the GitHub release notes describe as boosting throughput by more than 3x for long sequence lengths.

Jenny: Multiblock Attention — what is that mechanically?

Alex: It's a long-context kernel. Splits attention work into blocks that can run in parallel and avoids materializing the full attention matrix. So instead of computing one giant attention matrix that scales quadratically with sequence length, you break it into blocks that can be processed independently and then combined.

Jenny: That's basically what FlashAttention does at a kernel level.

Alex: Same family. FlashAttention is the canonical version. Multiblock Attention is TRT-LLM's paged-attention-aware version of that idea — it knows about the block structure of the KV cache and splits work accordingly.

Jenny: Okay. Continuous batching — same as vLLM?

Alex: Same primitive, different name. They call it in-flight batching. Introl describes it as adding new requests to running batches at each generation step, processing context and generation phases together to maximize GPU utilization. Same idea as vLLM. At every decode iteration, finished requests leave the batch and new prefill requests join.

Jenny: So why call it something different?

Alex: Mostly just terminology. But there's a real architectural distinction worth pulling out. In vLLM, the continuous batching scheduler is a Python loop sitting outside the compiled kernels. In TRT-LLM, it's implemented inside the same compiled engine that runs the matmuls.

Jenny: So the scheduler decisions can be fused with kernel launches.

Alex: Yes. That's the structural advantage of doing it inside the engine. The CUDA Graph capture we talked about? You can capture graphs that include scheduler bookkeeping. You couldn't do that if the scheduler was a Python loop.

Jenny: Okay, that's a real difference. Let's hit the third bucket. Arithmetic.

Alex: Arithmetic and serial-chain. Two big things here. Quantization and speculative decoding. Quantization first. The developer hub lists FP8 and NVFP4 quantization as headline features. Introl ranks them by use case. FP8 is the best performance-accuracy tradeoff on Hopper and Blackwell. INT8 SmoothQuant is the good alternative on Ada GPUs. INT4 AWQ and GPTQ are for maximum compression — and crucially, INT4 AWQ excels in small-batch scenarios, batch size four or fewer, where inference becomes memory-bound.

Jenny: Wait, why is small-batch memory-bound?

Alex: Because at small batch you're not amortizing the weight-loading cost over many tokens. The weights have to come from HBM into SRAM for every forward pass, and if your batch only has four tokens, that weight bandwidth is your whole bottleneck. Quantizing the weights from FP16 to INT4 cuts the weight-loading bandwidth by 4x.

Jenny: So at large batch, you're compute-bound, the math dominates and quantization helps less. At small batch, you're memory-bound, weight loading dominates and aggressive quantization helps a lot.

Alex: Exactly. And the release notes have a beautiful concrete example — Falcon-180B on a single H200 GPU with INT4 AWQ. A model that does not fit at FP16 fitting on one card after AWQ.

Jenny: That's the use case. Memory-pressure escape hatch.

Alex: Memory-pressure escape hatch. NVFP4 is the newer Blackwell-era format. It's more aggressive than FP8, less aggressive than INT4. Introl ties it to the December 2025 releases. And the wide-EP blog uses NVFP4 inside the AlltoAll combine to compress per-token expert outputs in flight, which we'll get to.

Jenny: So the mental model — FP8 is throughput tier on current Hopper and Blackwell, NVFP4 is the next step down for memory-bound and Blackwell-specific paths, INT4 AWQ is the small-batch escape hatch.

Alex: Three layers of the same compression stack. Each one has Tensor Core support on the hardware that lists it. That last point matters a lot — quantization without hardware support doesn't help you, it hurts you because you're emulating in software.

Jenny: Right. Speculative decoding. Walk me through it. I know the basic idea — small drafter model proposes tokens, big model verifies them in parallel.

Alex: That's the idea. The TRT-LLM landing page enumerates six supported drafting methods. N-Gram, Lookahead, Medusa, Eagle, Eagle3, and MTP — Multi-Token Prediction.

Jenny: Six. Why six?

Alex: Because they trade off differently on different workloads. N-Gram is dirt simple — you look at recent tokens and predict the next ones from a frequency table. Lookahead is structured around generating multiple tokens per step using algorithmic patterns. Medusa adds extra prediction heads to the model itself. Eagle and Eagle3 are tree-based draft models with dynamic structures. MTP — Multi-Token Prediction — has a separate small draft head that predicts multiple future tokens.

Jenny: So the choice depends on how much you trust the drafter and how much workload-specific structure you can exploit.

Alex: That's the core tradeoff. Introl gives a magnitude estimate that holds across speculative variants in general — 1.5 to 2x speedup for compatible workloads. The bottleneck speculative decoding attacks is the autoregressive serial chain. Each accepted draft token saves one decode step. So the gain depends on accept rate, which depends on the drafter and the workload.

Jenny: Compatible workloads. What's a non-compatible workload?

Alex: Anything where the drafter accept rate is low. If your drafter is bad relative to the target — wrong distribution, wrong domain — then you're paying drafter cost for tokens that get rejected, and the speedup collapses. Or even goes negative.

Jenny: So this is one of those features that looks like a free win and isn't.

Alex: It's never free. The 1.5 to 2x is for compatible workloads, latency-sensitive applications where time-to-completion matters. If your batch is huge and you're already throughput-bound, speculative decoding might not help at all because you don't have spare GPU compute to verify drafts.

Jenny: That's the subtle one. You need spare verification capacity.

Alex: You need the GPU to be sitting idle some of the time during decode. Which is true at low batch and low concurrency, false at high batch and high concurrency.

Jenny: Okay. Let me try to pull the segment two summary together. CPU-side wins — CUDA Graph capture, CUDA Graph padding for up to 22%, Overlap Scheduler. Memory bandwidth wins — kernel fusion that keeps activations in registers and SRAM, paged KV with hand-tuned attention kernels, FP8 attention on Hopper and Blackwell, Multiblock Attention more than 3x on long sequences. Arithmetic wins — FP8 throughput tier, NVFP4 Blackwell-aggressive tier, INT4 AWQ small-batch escape, six speculative decoders for 1.5 to 2x on compatible workloads.

Alex: That's the whole stack. And there's one operating point I want to drop in for calibration. The landing page reports H200 achieves nearly 12,000 tokens per second on Llama2-13B, and 10,000 tokens per second at 100 milliseconds time-to-first-token on H100. The repo highlights 24,000 tokens per second on Meta Llama 3 with 3x faster AllReduce via NVSwitch and TensorRT-LLM MultiShot. Vendor benchmarks under specific configs. Treat them as upper bounds.

Jenny: Always upper bounds. Okay. So now we've covered the standard optimization stack. What's the part that's distinctly TRT-LLM?

Alex: The wide expert parallelism work. This is segment three and it's the single biggest TRT-LLM differentiator the cited sources actually support. The developer hub flags it directly — "wide expert parallelism and disaggregated serving." And the detailed engineering writeup lives in the wide-EP part-3 tech blog, which is more specific than any other source I have.

Jenny: Okay. What's the problem?

Alex: The problem is mixture-of-experts models. DeepSeek R1 is the canonical target. Hundreds of experts. Each token gets routed to a small subset of experts — usually two, sometimes more. To run a model like that on cluster-shaped hardware, you have to distribute experts across many GPUs. So instead of every replica holding every expert — which doesn't fit — you shard experts across GPUs and route tokens to whichever GPU holds the right experts.

Jenny: And then you have to ship the tokens to the GPU that has the experts, and ship the results back.

Alex: Right. Which makes the all-to-all communication step between MLP-down and MLP-up the dominant cost. Tokens fly across the network to the GPU holding their assigned expert, the expert does its computation, then results fly back across the network to the original GPU. That all-to-all is the gate.

Jenny: How is this different from regular tensor parallelism's all-reduce?

Alex: All-reduce is symmetric — every GPU sends the same shape to every other GPU. All-to-all is also symmetric in that sense, but the payloads carry token-specific routing decisions. Each token has different destinations based on which experts it was routed to. So the kernel has to handle dispatch, not just reduction.

Jenny: Got it. The kernel is doing routing on the wire.

Alex: Routing on the wire. And the wide-EP blog walks through three big optimizations. AlltoAll redesign. Programmatic Dependent Launch — PDL. And MLA-specific kernel surgery. Let's start with the AlltoAll redesign because it's the most architecturally interesting.

Jenny: Go.

Alex: TRT-LLM redesigned and reimplemented AlltoAll so that multiple data fields fuse into single operations. Hidden states, scaling factors, token routing information — all packed into one all-to-all instead of three. The blog says this reduces kernel launch overhead, which is critical in wide-EP scenarios.

Jenny: Wait, you're saying the naive way is to do three separate all-to-alls? One for hidden states, one for scales, one for routing?

Alex: That's the naive way. And every all-to-all is a synchronization point and a kernel launch. If you can fuse them, you cut your collective communication by 3x and your launch overhead by 3x.

Jenny: That's a lot. Okay.

Alex: Then on the combine phase — where each expert's outputs flow back to the requesting tokens — TRT-LLM temporarily quantizes the per-token expert outputs to NVFP4. Sends the compact representation across GPUs. And dequantizes back to the original dtype on the receiving side.

Jenny: Wait, hold on. They're quantizing on the wire?

Alex: Quantizing on the wire. NVFP4 is 4 bits. So you're sending one quarter the bytes you would for FP16. The blog cites PRs 7155 and 7898 as the implementation.

Jenny: That's beautiful. Network is the bottleneck, FP4 cuts the wire payload by 4x, and the quant-dequant cost is absorbed on either side because Blackwell is already cheap on FP4.

Alex: Exactly. The receiving GPU is already heavily optimized for FP4, so the dequantization is essentially free. You move the bottleneck off the network and onto the GPU, where you have spare capacity.

Jenny: Okay, that's the kind of optimization that's only possible if you control the kernels, the network layer, and the precision support all the way down.

Alex: Which is the whole NVIDIA stack thesis. They own the GPU, the network, the kernels, the compiler. They can co-design.

Jenny: Okay. PDL.

Alex: Programmatic Dependent Launch. CUDA-level mechanism that enables kernels to overlap execution within the same CUDA stream. The blog has profiling examples showing three kernels — moeComputeRouteKernel, computeCountAndIndiceDevice, computeCumsumDevice — executing strictly sequentially without PDL, then overlapping with PDL enabled.

Jenny: So this is — wait, this is finer-grained than the Overlap Scheduler we talked about?

Alex: Much finer. The Overlap Scheduler is host-level. CPU runs scheduler bookkeeping for iteration N+1 while GPU runs iteration N. PDL is device-level. It's the device hardware saying "kernel B can begin once kernel A produces enough of its output." Kernel A doesn't have to fully finish.

Jenny: That's like out-of-order execution at the kernel granularity.

Alex: That's a perfect analogy. CPUs do this in hardware with reorder buffers and dependency tracking. PDL is the GPU version — the host tells the device about producer-consumer dependencies, and the device hardware actually starts the consumer kernel as soon as the relevant data is available.

Jenny: Okay so we have AlltoAll fusion plus FP4 on the wire, plus PDL for finer-grained overlap. What's the third one?

Alex: MLA. Multi-head Latent Attention. This is DeepSeek's specific attention variant. It compresses keys and values into a smaller latent space, which saves memory. But the price is a more complex Q/K/V layout — Q and K are divided into two parts: with RoPE and without RoPE.

Jenny: Two parts because RoPE rotational position embedding only applies to part of the dimension.

Alex: Right. And the naive implementation of MLA has to concat Q, K, and V from those two parts. PR 6538 eliminated that concat. Saved copy operations. Significantly improved prefill latency in wide-EP scenarios.

Jenny: That's just kernel-level surgery.

Alex: Pure kernel-level surgery. And there's more. There's a quantization story specific to MLA — FP8 Context FMHA. It quantizes query projections to FP8 to match an FP8 KV cache. Enables FP8 Tensor Core operations during prefill. Automatically enabled when an FP8 KV cache is selected. PRs 7610 and 7612.

Jenny: Wait. The KV cache is FP8 already, so they're matching the query precision to the cache precision.

Alex: Exactly. If the KV cache is FP8 and the query is FP16, you have to upcast the cache to FP16 — or downcast the query to FP8 — to do the attention math. They chose to downcast the query so the entire attention math runs on FP8 Tensor Cores.

Jenny: That's another co-design decision. You quantized the cache, now match the math.

Alex: One more on MLA. The LM head — the final projection that produces logits over the vocabulary — gets MTP LM Head Tensor Parallelism. Intra-node AllGather across 4 GPUs. 4-way TP to split the vocabulary weights. Then it collects local argmax logits before global reduction. PRs 7571 and 7891. Reduces weight loading time in memory-bound scenarios.

Jenny: That's clever. The vocabulary projection is huge — DeepSeek has like 130,000 tokens — so the weight matrix is enormous. Splitting it 4 ways means each GPU only loads a quarter of it.

Alex: Right. And the local argmax thing is the magic. Instead of sending all 32,000 logits per partition back to do a global argmax, each GPU does a local argmax over its slice and sends just the local winner. Then global reduce picks the global winner.

Jenny: That's a bandwidth optimization. Instead of an all-gather of 130K floats, you're doing an all-gather of 4 floats and 4 indices. Massive bandwidth reduction.

Alex: Massive. And there's still one more I want to mention. WO GEMM FP4 quantization. Quantizes the attention output projection — the matrix that maps attention output back to model dimension — to FP4. While maintaining a similar MTP accept rate.

Jenny: Why is the accept rate the metric there?

Alex: Because if you over-quantize a projection that's part of the speculative pipeline, you collapse the accept rate. And if you collapse the accept rate, your speculative decoding gives you no speedup — or negative speedup, because you're paying drafter cost for rejected tokens. So the constraint when quantizing inside a speculative pipeline isn't just "is the loss similar," it's "does the accept rate hold."

Jenny: That's a really interesting cross-coupling. Quantization choices feeding into speculative decoding choices.

Alex: That's the wide-EP picture. End-to-end. The whole flow — token routing through moeComputeRouteKernel, AlltoAll dispatch with fused fields, expert MLPs at FP8 or NVFP4, AlltoAll combine with NVFP4 on the wire and dequant on the receiver, then MLA plus MTP head with FP8 Context FMHA and 4-way LM-head TP. Every stage co-designed.

Jenny: Why is this a TRT-LLM differentiator and not, say, a vLLM differentiator?

Alex: Because the level of mechanism detail and the cadence of merged PRs against DeepSeek R1 as a target workload — this is where the kernel-level optimization is being landed first. SGLang has its own wide-EP work. vLLM has its own wide-EP work. But the wide-EP blog's concrete PR list and the level of fusion they're doing is what an interview answer should point to as the TRT-LLM differentiator.

Jenny: So the framing is: when the workload is a frontier MoE on Blackwell, TRT-LLM is where the optimization lands first.

Alex: That's the line. When the workload is a frontier MoE on Blackwell, TRT-LLM is where the kernel-level optimization is landing first. And the cited sources don't give me an engine-versus-engine table for wide-EP, but the directional claim is well supported.

Jenny: Okay. Let me ask the obvious follow-up. If TRT-LLM is so optimized, why isn't everyone running it? What's the catch?

Alex: That's segment four. The honest tradeoff. And the cleanest head-to-head data point in the cited sources is in introl's optimization guide. On Llama-70B on A100, comparative numbers — TRT-LLM peak throughput around 700 tokens per second, vLLM around 600 to 650. TTFT 35 to 50 milliseconds for TRT-LLM, 50 to 80 for vLLM. Short-sequence advantage 1.34x for TRT-LLM. Long-sequence TPOT advantage 2.72x for TRT-LLM. Setup complexity weeks for TRT-LLM, hours for vLLM.

Jenny: 1.34x to 2.72x performance, weeks instead of hours.

Alex: That's the tradeoff in one line. Weeks of build, validation, and calibration time, in exchange for that performance window. And introl is direct about when each engine wins. TRT-LLM is a strong fit when the team is committed to NVIDIA GPU infrastructure long-term, has CUDA and TensorRT experience, is running workloads where 20 to 40% performance improvement justifies weeks of optimization, is at production scale exceeding $50,000 monthly, or has sub-50-millisecond TTFT requirements.

Jenny: $50K monthly is the floor where weeks of engineering pays for itself.

Alex: That's introl's bar. And the inverse — TRT-LLM is a weak fit for rapid prototyping, teams without NVIDIA optimization expertise, multi-cloud requirements, small-scale deployments where vLLM suffices, and frequent model changes.

Jenny: Frequent model changes is the killer one. If you're swapping models every two weeks, you can't sink weeks into each build.

Alex: Right. The summary line from introl is: "Deploy TensorRT-LLM when maximizing hardware efficiency justifies engineering investment. Choose vLLM for faster time-to-production or when operating at smaller scale."

Jenny: Okay. So an honest decision framework for someone making this call.

Alex: Pick TRT-LLM when the deployment is large enough that 1.34x to 2.72x pays for weeks of build and calibration time. Or when the hardware is Blackwell or Blackwell Ultra and you want NVFP4 plus the wide-EP kernels. Or when the workload is a frontier MoE like DeepSeek R1 where the all-to-all and MLA optimizations land first. Or when TTFT below 50 milliseconds is a hard product constraint.

Jenny: Pick vLLM when?

Alex: When time-to-production matters more than peak throughput. When hardware diversity matters — multi-cloud, AMD, future flexibility. When the model lineup changes faster than a build pipeline can keep up with. Or when the deployment scale just doesn't justify the engineering tax.

Jenny: That's a real framework. And it doesn't pretend one engine is strictly better than the other.

Alex: It can't pretend that. They occupy different points on the engineering-investment-versus-performance curve. And honestly, the 1.34x to 2.72x numbers are introl's numbers for Llama-70B on A100. On Blackwell against the most recent vLLM with FlashAttention-3 and FP8, the gap will look different. Cite the framing, not the precise multiplier.

Jenny: That's important. The relative gap is going to keep moving as both projects ship.

Alex: Always. And there's one more orthogonal point I want to make for an interview. This isn't actually a one-engine choice for a real production fleet. Most NVIDIA-aligned shops will end up running both. TRT-LLM for the high-volume flagship workloads. vLLM for the long tail of internal models, experiments, and hardware footprints that don't yet have an optimized TRT-LLM path.

Jenny: That's the operationally honest framing. Not "which engine wins," but "what's the workload-shape allocation across engines."

Alex: Right. And then the infrastructure question becomes how to operate two engines side by side. Which is what NIM exists to absorb. Which is segment five.

Jenny: Okay. NIM. Walk me through it. What's the actual artifact?

Alex: I want to be careful here. The cited sources do not contain a description of NIM Factory's pipeline architecture, its validation matrix, or its container build internals. So I'm going to be precise about what's source-supported versus what's editorial extension.

Jenny: That's fair. What does the source say.
